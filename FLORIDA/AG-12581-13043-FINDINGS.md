# The Primary Source Arrives

*Prepared August 23, 2026; §§ 4a and 4b added the same day. On Auditor General Report No. 12581 (September 12, 1995) and Report No. 13043 (August 25, 1997), supplied by the Auditor General's office and scanned by that office on August 2, 2026. Both are 300-dpi Tesseract OCR of the supplied PDFs; page citations are to OCR page markers, which differ from the printed pagination. Full extractions are in `_working/AG-12581-extraction.md` and the 13043 pages under `_working/ocr/`. Companion to `AG-1998-SYSTEMS-REVIEW-FINDINGS.md`, which covers the four reports logged on August 11.*

Report 12581 is the document this project has been arguing about for months without having read. It is the source of the 95-percent utilization figure, and the source of nearly everything the press wrote in August 1995. Having it changes several claims, sharpens others, and requires one correction the archive will not enjoy.

---

## 1. The correction the archive has to make first

**The Auditor General did not measure 95 percent CPU utilization. A consultant did, and the Auditor General reported it.**

The archive has been writing sentences like "the structural gap that Auditor General Report 12581 later measured at 95–100% CPU utilization." That is wrong in its attribution. Paragraph 44 reads:

> "According to a report dated October 11, 1994, by an independent consultant hired by the Department, the FLORIDA System CPU was operating at approximately 95 percent capacity (i.e., a measure of the ability to process data) during most of the regular work day and during some peak times at 100 percent capacity." [12581, p. 53]

And the comparison figure is not an independent benchmark either:

> "In comparison, industry data obtained by the Department showed that the average commercial data center's CPU operates at about 65 percent capacity during an 11-hour day window. While estimates for practical maximum CPU capacity may vary, a CPU should have the capacity to allow for unexpected increases in the workload." [12581, p. 53]

Both numbers are the Department's own — one from a consultant it hired, one from industry data it gathered. The Auditor General repeated them without independent verification and appended a caution that "estimates for practical maximum CPU capacity may vary."

**This is not a retreat. It is a stronger position.** A figure the audited agency itself produced, against a benchmark the audited agency itself selected, is harder for a skeptic to dismiss than an auditor's estimate would be — the agency had every incentive to make the comparison flattering and could not. But the archive must say who measured it, and must stop writing "the Auditor General measured."

A second, independent utilization figure appears elsewhere and should be cited alongside:

> "Department records show that the ES9021/900 CPU ran at approximately 95 percent capacity during regular workday hours." [12581, p. 29]

## 2. The response-time series, dated — and how the numbers were achieved

The archive has carried "minutes → 3 seconds" as a bare progression. Paragraph 47 gives the series with dates, and gives the mechanism, which matters more than the numbers:

| Date | Average response time | Source |
|---|---|---|
| June 1992 | "in minutes" | Department records |
| January 1994 | eight seconds | Department records |
| June 1994 | four seconds | Department records |
| December 1994 | three seconds | Department records |

> "In an effort to alleviate the prime time performance problems and maintain an acceptable level of system performance, the Department implemented, in June 1994, a plan to limit the maximum number of users allowed on the System at any one point in time, which included scheduling staff to work off-peak hours (including nights and Saturdays)." [12581, p. 55]

**Read the table against that sentence.** The improvement from eight seconds to four is bracketed by the introduction of the user-limiting plan. The system got faster in part because the Department stopped letting people use it — caseworkers were rationed by concurrent-user cap and pushed onto nights and Saturdays. The three-second figure is not a measure of a system that was fixed. It is a measure of a system whose demand was suppressed to fit its capacity.

That is the single most useful thing in this report for the archive's argument, and no press account carried it.

The report also states the consequence in the auditor's own voice:

> "the FLORIDA System's response times and capacity requirements continue to contribute to delays in the Department's ability to process data within the FLORIDA System, as similarly noted in audit report No. 12061, paragraphs 49 through 59. Such delays have caused the FDHRS to extend employee work hours, limit the number of PA and CSE users on the System, and upgrade the System's central processing unit." [12581, p. 51]

## 3. The upgrade did not fix it

The Department replaced the production processor during the audit period, and the auditors were still there to see the result.

- The prior production CPU, an **IBM ES9021/900**, "had been deemed inadequate by the Department to handle the projected processing requirements" and ran at about 95 percent capacity during regular workday hours [12581, p. 29].
- Its replacement, an **IBM ES9021/982** with eight processors, was installed by IBM on **April 22 and 23, 1995**, at a total cost transcribed as **$5,438,079.75** [12581, pp. 29, 55].
- The Department's own assessment of the upgrade before it happened: it "will not satisfy the total need, but will improve the system performance by 20 percent" [12581, p. 29].
- Initial testing showed response times falling "from the two to four second range down to the half-second range or below" [12581, p. 55].
- **"However, the CPU, with six of the eight processors, was running between 90 and 97.5 percent capacity as of May 1, 1995."** [12581, p. 55]

Eight days after installation, on six of eight processors, the new machine was already back in the same utilization band as the old one. The archive should quote that sentence next to the 95-percent figure whenever it uses either, because together they establish that the problem was not a machine that was too small but a workload that had been sized wrong and kept outrunning every remedy.

**One coincidence to note and not to build on.** The upgrade cost $5,438,079.75 in 1995. Press accounts describe a 1993 grand jury examining whether HRS improperly favored IBM on a **$5.1 million** hardware upgrade. Those are different transactions two years apart, and nothing here connects them. The resemblance is worth remembering when the grand jury materials arrive; it is not evidence of anything now.

## 4. The as-built machine in 1995 — and the 3090 is still there

> "The FLORIDA System's hardware configuration consists of an IBM ES3090/600J central processing unit (CPU) which is utilized for training, development, and data communications, and an IBM ES9021/982 CPU on which the production data is processed." [12581, p. 27]

The 3090 survived into 1995, demoted from production to training, development, and data communications. Report 13287 shows it gone by 1998, replaced by a three-machine Sysplex. The archive can now trace the processor lineage across three documented states rather than inferring it.

Other 1995 facts:

- **Approximately 16,000 network devices** as of January 1995 — "terminals, workstations, printers, and controllers" — across the Central Office and 15 districts [12581, p. 27].
- **37 PA and CSE modules** as of May 4, 1995 [12581, p. 15]. Report 13287 gives 33 as of March 1998.
- RACF for file and program security, with additional security inside the application [12581, p. 27].

- **561 PA and 107 CSE top-priority outstanding problems** as of January 20, 1995 [12581, p. 61].

The report gives no database count, no OSAM or VSAM figures, and no data volume. The ITN 03F12GC1 numbers remain unsubstituted.

## 4a. Kastner's reading of the 1995 configuration

*Recorded August 23, 2026. The three numbered observations are Peter Kastner's, stated in his words and then assessed. He read the hardware sentence in paragraph 9 as a practitioner rather than as a records item, and two of the three readings hold up and change what the utilization figures mean. The third does not, and the reason it fails is itself useful.*

### The observations as stated

1. The as-bid 3090/300 had already been upgraded to a top-of-the-line 3090-600.
2. The 16,000-device communications load was offloaded from the ES9021 onto the 3090-600. The training and development loads are trivial compared with 16,000 terminals' traffic.
3. The 3090-600 and the ES9021 had to be connected in order to move the communications traffic to and from the ES9021. Sysplex is inferred.

### Observation 1 — supported, and larger than "upgraded" suggests

EDS proposed a **3090/300E**; the winning demonstration was run on a **3090/400E**, a discrepancy the hearing officer noted and let stand ([89-0003BID](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1989/89000003.PDF) ¶¶71–72). Report 12581 finds an **ES3090/600J** in 1995 [p. 27].

That is two steps, not one. The 600J is a six-way machine announced in 1989, and IBM restricted the upgrade path to it: "only 3090-600S or 600E models installed by yesterday may be upgraded to the 3090-600J" ([Tech Monitor, on IBM's late-life 3090 kickers](https://www.techmonitor.ai/technology/ibm_improves_batch_performance_seeks_to_trip_up_leasers_with_16_model_3090_kicker)). So reaching a 600J from a 300E required first doubling the engine count to a 600E or 600S and then taking the J-model conversion. The 3090 was a field-upgradeable family ([Chilton Computing](https://www.chilton-computing.org.uk/ccd/mainframes/p009.htm)), so this may all have happened to the same box.

**What the archive can say:** the processor delivered in production was, by 1995, at least twice the engine count of the machine EDS proposed and a generation beyond it. **What it cannot say:** when the upgrades happened, at whose cost, or whether they were field conversions or replacements. Report 12581 does not date the 3090's arrival at 600J and no other document in the archive does either. That is a specific, answerable question for the APD file, which had to approve federal participation in hardware purchases.

### Observation 2 — supported by inference, and it is the one that matters

Paragraph 9 reads: the ES3090/600J "is utilized for training, development, and data communications, and an IBM ES9021/982 CPU on which the production data is processed" [p. 27].

Kastner's point is a sizing argument, and it is sound. Training and development workloads on a shop this size do not occupy a six-way top-of-line 3090. Sixteen thousand terminals, workstations, printers, and controllers across a central office and fifteen districts do. The listed function that justifies the machine is data communications; the other two are passengers.

**The analytical consequence is the important part, and the archive has been understating its own case.** If the network host is a separate machine, then every utilization figure in this report describes a production processor that had *already been relieved of terminal traffic*:

- the consultant's 95 percent regular-day and 100 percent peak figures of October 11, 1994;
- the roughly 95 percent recorded for the ES9021/900;
- the 90 to 97.5 percent measured on the ES9021/982 on May 1, 1995.

Those are the numbers for the application host alone. The full machine was that plus a six-way 3090 carrying the network. The archive's sentence should not be "the CPU ran at 95 percent" but "the production CPU ran at 95 percent with the communications workload hosted elsewhere."

It also reframes the $5,438,079.75 upgrade. The Department bought a bigger application host and predicted a 20 percent improvement it did not fully get; nothing in the report suggests the network host was touched.

**One correction to the phrasing.** The report says the 3090 "is utilized for" those functions. It does not say the communications load was moved off the ES9021, and the split may have been the design from the beginning rather than a remedy. The archive should write *hosted on* rather than *offloaded to* unless a document establishes a transfer. The consequence for the utilization figures is the same either way.

### Observation 3 — the interconnect is real, the Sysplex is not

The two machines certainly had to be coupled; SNA sessions terminating on a network host have to reach the application host somehow. But **Sysplex should not be carried back from 1998 into 1995**, and the hardware forbids the strong form of it.

Report 12581 never uses the word. It appears only in Report 13287, describing the 1998 estate — an ES9021-982, a 9672-R83, and a 9672-R44, "linked together in a Sysplex environment," with no 3090 present. A Parallel Sysplex requires a coupling facility, and IBM's own documentation restricts the coupling-facility control code to "an IBM Enterprise System/9000 (ES/9000) 9021 711-based processor," an IBM 9672, or an IBM 9674 ([z/OS: Coupling facilities](https://www.ibm.com/docs/en/zos/3.1.0?topic=planning-coupling-facilities)). **A 3090 of any model is not on that list.** In 1995 the ordinary mechanism for this pairing would have been channel-to-channel connection with VTAM cross-domain routing, and possibly IMS MSC or ISC links between the two systems — none of which the report describes.

**And this closes a loop.** The ES9021 Model 982 *is* a 711-based machine, announced in 1993 as the most powerful single-image commercial processor IBM then offered ([Computer Museum, ES/9000 and System/390 chronology](https://www.computermuseum.org.uk/fixed_pages/sys390.pdf)), and IBM's Sysplex documentation uses "9021 … model 982" as its worked example. So the 982 could join the 1998 Sysplex and the 3090-600J could not. The Sysplex appears in the record at precisely the moment the 3090 disappears from it. That is not a coincidence to assert as causation, but it is consistent, and it means the 1995-to-1998 transition was a topology change forced in part by hardware eligibility rather than a simple capacity purchase.

### What this adds up to

The state bid a three-way 3090/300E as the production machine. By May 1995 it was running an eight-processor ES9021/982 — a machine IBM marketed as the most powerful single-image commercial processor available — for the application workload alone, with a six-way 3090-600J alongside it carrying the network, and the application host was still at 90 to 97.5 percent with six of eight engines active.

For scale, IBM claimed the six-processor ES/9000 Model 900 delivered "1.7 to 1.9 times the processor performance of the 3090 Model 600J in commercial applications" ([Tech Monitor on the ES/3090 line](https://www.techmonitor.ai/technology/ibmes3_9_0)), and the 982 sat above the 900 in the same family. Precise MIPS ratios against a 3090/300E are not established here and should not be invented. But the direction and the rough magnitude are not in doubt: the delivered production processor was several times the capacity of the one proposed, and it was still saturated.

**That is the quantitative form of the archive's central claim.** The transaction-volume assumptions behind the 1988 sizing were not off by a margin that a prudent headroom allowance would have absorbed. They were off by a multiple large enough that two generations of processor upgrade, a separate host for the network, a cap on concurrent users, and caseworkers moved onto nights and Saturdays still left the machine at ninety-plus percent. The archive should make that argument in those terms rather than resting on the single 95 percent figure.

**Status of this section:** observations 1 and 2 are inference from a primary source, labeled as such. Observation 3 is corrected. None of it is in the published papers yet.

## 4b. Growth against the bid, and the question it raises

*Recorded August 23, 2026. Kastner's observation and question, then analysis.*

### The observation as stated

> Even worse, the original production hosted 12,000 terminals. By 1995, user count had grown by a third to 16,000, requiring communications overhead offloading to a 3090-600J. The original bid capacity numbers bear little reality to what was built and needed. Why was the original bid constrained to an obviously inadequate computing platform?

### Two cautions before the archive uses the comparison

**The two figures are not the same unit, and the archive must not present them as one series.**

The 12,000 figure is press-sourced. It comes from the [Los Angeles Times of May 2, 1993](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-05-02-mn-30237-story.html), reported alongside 5 million transactions per day and 2.3 million welfare cases, and the addendum paper already flags it as "presumably derived from the RFP/contract" — a stated capacity target, not a count of installed equipment. **No document in this archive states an as-bid or as-installed terminal count.** RFP 88-74-BC would, and the archive does not have it.

The 16,000 figure is documentary and precise, and it is broader: "approximately 16,000 network devices consisting of terminals, workstations, printers, and controllers throughout the Department's Central Office and the 15 districts" [12581, p. 27]. Printers and controllers are in that number. Terminals alone are some unknown fraction of it.

So "grown by a third" compares a press-reported target for one class of device against a documented count of four classes. The direction of growth is almost certainly right — a system that went statewide in November 1992 and did not finish converting its caseload until April 1993 was not fully deployed when the 12,000 figure was published. But the archive cannot put 33 percent in print. **What it can print:** the design target most widely reported was 12,000 terminals, and by January 1995 the Department was operating roughly 16,000 network devices of all classes. That is a defensible sentence and it makes the same point.

The district count also moved. The README describes "11 service districts and 67 counties"; Report 12581 says fifteen districts [p. 27]. Worth reconciling in one pass — HRS reorganized its district structure more than once, and the archive appears to be carrying a figure from a different year without saying which.

### Why the bid was constrained to an inadequate platform

This is the right question and the record now supports a fuller answer than "the model's inputs were wrong." Five mechanisms, in descending order of how well the archive can evidence them.

**1. The contract was fixed price and it included the hardware.** Report 12581 states it plainly: "a fixed price, single vendor contract that provided for system development, hardware, a telecommunications network, training and conversion activities, system implementation, and facilities management" [p. 17]. That single sentence is most of the answer. Under a fixed-price contract that bundles the iron, every processor the bidder proposes comes out of the bidder's own margin. The bidder's incentive is not to size the platform correctly; it is to size it as small as it can defend during evaluation. Adequacy is a cost to the vendor and a benefit only to the buyer, and only later.

**2. Nothing in the procurement made adequacy a requirement.** The archive's structural claim — that the RFP specified functional completeness and acceptance criteria but no quantified throughput, response-time, availability, or utilization-headroom standard — remains inference from silence in the appellate record. But it is the necessary complement to point 1. A fixed-price hardware bundle with a binding performance standard forces the bidder to size honestly, because undersizing means paying to fix it. A fixed-price hardware bundle with **no** performance standard rewards undersizing with no exposure. The two features together, not either alone, produce the result.

**3. Price was scored; capacity was not.** Unisys lost by 4.4 points out of 350 on a bid $16.9 million higher nominal and $14.0 million higher in present value ([89-0003BID](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1989/89000003.PDF)). Whatever the technical merits, that margin tells any bidder reading the record afterward that proposing more machine is expensive and unrewarded. The evaluation did what it was designed to do.

**4. The undersizing was not, in the end, borne by the bidder — and the settlement proves it.** This is the part Report 13043 newly supplies. When the platform proved inadequate and more equipment had to be bought, the cost of that equipment became the substance of the litigation EDS brought and won:

| Settlement count | Amount |
|---|---|
| District Equipment | $19,446,676 |
| Central Processing Equipment | $13,188,325 |

[13043, p. 2]

Thirty-two and a half million dollars of hardware, settled in 1996, on top of $3,713,085 the Department had already paid for district equipment before the settlement [13043, p. 4]. Report 12656 describes roughly $30.3 million of FLORIDA mainframe equipment received between fiscal years 1991-92 and 1993-94. And the 1995 processor upgrade cost a further $5,438,079.75 [12581, p. 29].

**The fixed price did not hold on the one dimension where holding it mattered.** The equipment the original bid did not include was bought anyway, litigated over, and paid for by the State with federal participation. Whether EDS intended that outcome is not established and should not be asserted. What is established is that the structure permitted it: a bidder could win on a lean hardware proposal and be made whole later for the equipment the lean proposal omitted.

**5. The forecast error had an exogenous component, and that is the point rather than an excuse.** Report 12581 ¶45 lists among the causes "an unanticipated increase in the public assistance caseload" alongside "inadequacies and inefficiencies in the System's initial hardware, software, and application programming design" [p. 53]. Some of the volume growth was real-world — welfare caseloads rose across the country in the early 1990s and Florida's grew fast.

The archive should concede that squarely and then make the argument it enables. **A procurement with no specified headroom is one that cannot survive any forecast error, whatever its source.** The defect is not that HRS and EDS guessed the caseload wrong in 1988. Everyone guesses wrong. The defect is that the contract contained no instrument — no utilization ceiling, no sustained-throughput acceptance test, no headroom covenant — that would have made being wrong the vendor's problem instead of the State's. That is why the correction cost $32.6 million in a settlement rather than a warranty claim.

### The sentence this supports

The archive can now write, with citations: the platform was undersized because the procurement bundled hardware into a fixed price while specifying no performance standard to size it against, scored the resulting bid on cost, and provided no mechanism by which the consequences of undersizing would fall on the party who did it. Every incentive in the structure pointed at a smaller machine, and the machine that was eventually needed — a six-way 3090-600J for the network and an eight-way ES9021/982 for the application, still at ninety-plus percent — was bought over the following seven years at public expense.

**What would settle the open part.** RFP 88-74-BC Volume I would establish whether a performance standard existed, which is the load-bearing inference in the chain above. The APD file would establish when each hardware increment was approved and who paid. Both are already the archive's top acquisition targets and this strengthens the case for them.

Three years during which the State did not record what it owned. This is the same gap Report 12656 describes from the property side — roughly $30.3 million of FLORIDA mainframe equipment received before July 1, 1994 and not entered into SAMAS until FY 1994-95.

## 7. Two things nobody was looking for

### The 1995 protest repeats the 1989 pattern

In November 1994 HRS issued an RFP for FLORIDA application programming services. It announced an intent to award to **Unisys** on February 1, 1995. **Deloitte and Touche LLP** — one of EDS's own subcontractors on the original build — protested on February 13.

> "The Division's hearings officer found for Deloitte and Touche LLP on May 12, 1995, and exceptions to his findings were filed by the FDHRS and Unisys Corporation. Because the Secretary of FDHRS was a material witness in the case, he could not enter the final order. Accordingly, an attorney from the Office of the Governor was appointed, as a Substitute Secretary, to consider the exceptions filed and to enter the final order within 90 days of May 12, 1995. On August 10, 1995, the Substitute Secretary for FDHRS found that the agency had acted within the law in awarding Unisys Corporation the contract." [12581, p. 25]

A DOAH hearing officer finds for the protester; the agency side enters a final order the other way. That is the 1988–89 shape again, with the parties reversed — Unisys is now the incumbent-favoured winner and the protester loses on the agency's exceptions. Meanwhile, on February 22, 1995, before any of it was resolved, the Secretary declared an emergency purchase citing "public health, safety, and welfare," and a Unisys contract was executed February 28 [12581, p. 25].

The archive should be careful here. This is a different procurement, a different protester, and a different legal posture, and the resemblance is structural rather than evidentiary. But it is the second documented instance of a Florida hearing officer being overruled on this system, and the memoir should have it.

### Three bidders, and the subcontractor list

> "After receiving proposals from three companies, through the evaluation process, the Department selected Electronic Data Systems Federal Corporation (EDS), located in Herndon, Virginia, as the prime contractor on November 28, 1988. The contract with EDS, signed by both parties in May 1989, was a fixed price, single vendor contract that provided for system development, hardware, a telecommunications network, training and conversion activities, system implementation, and facilities management." [12581, p. 17]

**Three proposals, not two.** The archive knows EDS and Unisys. There was a third bidder and the archive does not know who it was. That is a new, answerable question — the DOAH record or the procurement file should name it.

EDS's subcontractors are named: **IBM, Deloitte and Touche (formerly Touche Ross), IV-D Systems Inc., MIS Software Development Inc. (MSD), and Florida State University** [12581, p. 17]. The archive has treated this as an EDS/IBM pairing; it was a five-way team with a state university in it.
