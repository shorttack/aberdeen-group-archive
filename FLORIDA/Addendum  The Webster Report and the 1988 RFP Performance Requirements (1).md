## Part I — Webster's August 1995 special master report

### Availability: not online, anywhere

No PDF, transcript, or full text of William H. Webster's August 1995 special master report exists on the open web. Checked: Google/Scholar, Casetext, CaseMine, Justia, FindLaw, Florida appellate ACIS dockets, the Leon County Clerk's online case system, the Florida State Archives online catalog, and the Wayback Machine.

The only substantive public rendering is the [Tampa Bay Times, Aug. 16, 1995 account](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1995/08/16/faulty-computer-could-cost-state-more/), which quotes the report directly: "EDS did not warrant that it would provide a perfect or maintenance-free computer system"; "it functioned properly, albeit imperfectly, and complied with the terms of the contract"; "EDS did not share this discovery with HRS"; "Good faith and fair dealing required EDS to disclose the erroneous modeling assumption to HRS."

This is the most useful find of the session. The Florida Auditor General issued:

## Part II — The original RFP performance requirements

### Direct availability: none

The two-volume May 1988 RFP is not digitized. Neither the RFP nor its performance specifications appear in the appellate record, which quotes only § 7.11 verbatim ([631 So. 2d 353](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1895493/state-dhrs-v-eds-federal-corp/)). No copy is indexed in the Florida State Archives catalog. The realistic custodians are DCF procurement/records management, the Leon County case file exhibits (the RFP was certainly an exhibit in 1992 CA 003618), and the HHS/FNS Advance Planning Document file.

### What can be reconstructed

**Stated system capacity targets** (as reported at the time, presumably derived from the RFP/contract): 5 million transactions per day, 2.3 million welfare cases, 12,000 terminals ([LA Times, May 2, 1993](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1993-05-02-mn-30237-story.html)).

**Actual delivered performance**, from Auditor General Report 12581 as reported: response times averaging **minutes** in June 1992; 8 seconds January 1994; 4 seconds June 1994 after shifting batch work to nights and Saturdays; 3 seconds December 1994. CPU utilization of approximately 95 percent during most of the regular work day and 100 percent at some peak times — figures the Auditor General did not measure. They come from a report dated October 11, 1994 by an independent consultant the Department hired, quoted at Report 12581 ¶44; the roughly 65 percent commercial-data-center comparison is industry data the Department itself obtained. A $5.4 million CPU upgrade roughly five months before the audit produced only about a 20% improvement ([Tampa Bay Times, Sept. 15, 1995](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1995/09/15/hrs-supercomputer-can-t-hack-it-report-says/)).

**Federal funding scale**: Florida drew $184.87 million in federal automated-welfare-system funds FY1984–92 ($71.59M enhanced, $113.28M regular), with FAMIS development continuing through FY1994 at an estimated $13.5 million remaining and a mainframe upgrade projected at $5.6 million ([GAO/AIMD-94-52FS](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-94-52FS/html/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-94-52FS.htm)). The $5.6 million projection and the $5.4 million actual upgrade are almost certainly the same procurement.

### Correction: the RFP did carry quantified performance requirements

This section previously reasoned that there probably were no enforceable performance requirements — an inference from the appellate record's silence and from the absence of any service-level metric in the federal framework the RFP had to satisfy. **That inference was wrong.** Florida Auditor General Report No. 12061, issued May 4, 1993 — audit supervised by Jonathan Ingram, audit made by Tina Greene, Auditor General Charles L. Lester — states the contract terms directly: response times were required to fall within 1 to 8 seconds at least 95 percent of the time, measured every 15 minutes at a random day and workstation on a monthly basis, and availability was required to be at least 97 percent over rolling 30-day periods (Report No. 12061 ¶¶49–59). Florida wrote a two-part quantified performance standard, with a measurement protocol, into a contract the federal framework required nothing of the kind for. The correction is worth stating plainly because it moves the indictment: the defect was not a missing standard but an unenforced one.

The RFP's requirements framework was not freely designed by HRS. To obtain 90% federal match, the system had to conform to the federal **FAMIS** standard and the AFDC **Automated Application Processing and Information Retrieval System (AAPIRS) Guide**, with the state's Advance Planning Document approved in advance and required to contain a requirements analysis, information flows and input/output formats, security and interface requirements, resource projections, a cost-benefit analysis of alternatives, a cost-distribution plan, and "an implementation plan with charts of development events, testing description, **proposed acceptance criteria**, and backup and fallback procedures" ([45 CFR § 205.37](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/205.37); [§ 205.38](https://www.govregs.com/regulations/expand/title45_chapterII_part205_section205.50)).

Note what that list contains and what it omits. It mandates *functional* completeness, compatibility, security, and documented acceptance criteria — but nothing about throughput, response time, availability, or utilization headroom. GAO identified this defect at the moment FAMIS was created, warning that the FAMIS requirements "do not contain sufficiently specific performance standards for evaluating the quality of State developed systems" and had never been pilot tested ([GAO/HRD-81-119](https://www.gao.gov/products/hrd-81-119)). GAO repeated the systemic critique a decade later in [IMTEC-92-29](https://www.gao.gov/products/imtec-92-29), finding that HHS and USDA "generally do not provide effective oversight or monitoring, resulting in costly automated system problems."

### The contract modification that removed the acceptance gates

The standard did not disappear on its own; it was modified. Report 12061 documents an **October 4, 1991 Memorandum of Understanding** between the Department and EDS with four operative terms (Report No. 12061 ¶57):

1. Benchmark testing was to be performed after the IBM hardware upgrades.
2. EDS was **released from providing evidence of successful benchmark tests** for the implementation stages already completed.
3. **Liquidated damages for benchmark-test delays were waived.**
4. Revised response times **were to be developed** — and were not developed before the contract's May 31, 1992 termination.

The auditor declined to characterize the result, and the refusal is itself evidence: "the legal effect of this modification is unknown and is currently the subject of litigation" (¶57). Read alongside the Department's own account — that EDS "postponed and rescheduled these deliverables, thereby delaying the tests, until they were useless" (Report No. 12061, Exhibit E, response to Finding #5) — the sequence is: a numeric standard written, its evidentiary requirement released for work already done, its penalty waived, its replacement never drafted, and the contract terminated eight months later.

This, rather than an absent standard, resolves the apparent paradox in Webster's ruling. A system running at ninety-five percent CPU with minute-scale response times "functioned properly, albeit imperfectly, and complied with the terms of the contract" because by the time performance was in dispute the contract's own acceptance machinery had been amended out of usable form — no benchmark evidence owed for completed stages, no liquidated damages for delay, and no revised response-time figures ever agreed. The capacity model remained an *assumption* underlying both parties' expectations rather than a tested *warranty*. Webster could therefore find simultaneously that EDS met the contract and that EDS breached the duty of good faith by concealing the modeling error. Whether the October 1991 MOU is read as the state bargaining away its remedy or as EDS extracting a release, the archive cannot say on this record — the auditor could not either, and said so.

For a 1988 procurement of this scale, that is the structural indictment: the commercial OLTP world already priced sizing risk through audited throughput benchmarks and utilization-headroom acceptance gates, while the federally shaped state-welfare procurement template did not require them. Florida did add them, and then let the gates go unclosed.

### What to ask for, specifically

When requesting the RFP from DCF or the court file, ask by section rather than for the whole document — the performance content, which Report 12061 ¶¶49–59 establishes exists, will sit in Volume I technical requirements under headings such as system sizing/capacity, response time standards, availability, acceptance testing, and system performance monitoring, plus any liquidated-damages or service-credit provisions. Also request the **Advance Planning Document and APD Updates submitted to HHS/ACF and USDA/FNS**, which by regulation must state the requirements analysis and proposed acceptance criteria and are held federally as well as by the state — often the easier of the two files to obtain.
