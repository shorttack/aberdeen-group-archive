## How to read and cite this note

1. **The body of the complaint (pp. 1–36) is EDS's pleading.** Every factual statement in it is an allegation by an interested party. Several are expressly made "upon information and belief." Cite them as "EDS alleged (Compl. ¶N)" and never as fact.
2. **Exhibits A–E are documents attached by EDS.** Their contents are documentary: the standard contract, the MOU and its amendment, the Wewerka letter, the HRS payment letter, the IBM installment contract, and the RFP excerpts. The fact that EDS selected them is itself a choice of advocacy. They are still the first copies of these instruments the archive holds.
3. **Page numbers are PDF pages** (1–79). Complaint paragraphs are given as ¶N, and MOU paragraphs as MOU ¶N.
4. **OCR caveat.** The text is technically complete but has not been verified for citation. Problem areas:
   - **pp. 42–43:** contract signature pages, flagged by tesseract.
   - **pp. 57–60:** installment-contract fine print, heavily garbled.
   - **pp. 72–79:** two-column RFP excerpts, heavily garbled.
   - **MOU model numbers:** the OCR renders the 600J inconsistently as "6007," "6003" and "600J."

   **Every quotation and dollar figure below must be checked against the page image before it goes into a paper.** Figures marked † are on pages with known OCR problems.

**Amendment One (p. 52), dated "as of October 18, 1991."** It rewrites the third sentence of MOU ¶1A so that "either the IBM M720 unit or the used IBM 600J" is operational by November 4, 1991, and "the remaining unit" by November 20, 1991. The signature block is partly illegible.

**The date discrepancy, resolved in substance.** The archive carries two dates in parallel: "October 4, 1991" (12061 ¶57) and "on or about October 18, 1991" (presentment ¶49). The MOU is dated October 4, 1991. Amendment One is dated October 18, 1991. *Agent inference:* the grand jury's date most likely refers to the amended instrument. No paper needs to keep carrying two dates for the MOU.

**The MOU and the presentment's reading of it.** The presentment states that under the MOU "all performance requirements that EDS was responsible for were waived" (¶49). On its face the document is narrower than that:
- The preamble preserves every provision not expressly modified.
- ¶1D keeps a capacity test, measured against the RFP caseload.
- ¶1F keeps a tuning obligation through May 31, 1992.
- ¶2E keeps TDR and PPR work.
- The benchmark release in ¶3A is **conditional** on installing the upgrade and delivering the code tapes.

What the MOU actually did was remove the benchmark as a payment deliverable for Tasks 12–15, bar liquidated damages for benchmark delays (¶3B), and, through the Wewerka letter (§ 1.2), set aside the RFP response-time categories pending a new methodology. In practice that may amount to what the grand jury described. The archive should still state the document's terms and the jury's reading separately. The main report § 5 already frames the jury as reading the MOU "more sharply than the auditor would"; the text now allows a third, documentary layer.

**Who sought the exit.** The MOU says twice that termination and non-renewal were "at EDS's request" (pp. 44, 48). The complaint goes further: "at EDS's strong urging, HRS agreed not to exercise its unilateral option to extend" (¶22). This is EDS's own admission against interest, in a pleading. It bears directly on **row 36c** (Kastner's exit-sequence synthesis). That row's component that EDS sought to leave by October 1991 moves from inference to documentary. Its motive component, the March 1991 internal discovery, remains inference and press-sourced.

**The 65/35 split and "$8.9 million."** EDS alleged that it paid "approximately $8.9 million, or 65 percent of the cost" (¶16), against an "$11.6 million computer upgrade" (¶18). The MOU sets the 1B cap at $11,615,000, with 1E costs on top. *Agent arithmetic:* 65 percent of $11,615,000 alone is about $7.55 million, but 65 percent of $11,615,000 plus $2,046,500 is about $8.88 million. That suggests EDS's $8.9 million counts the one-time 1E items and excludes the recurring charges. This is inference and should not be printed as fact. The presentment's "over 11 million dollars" (¶49) matches the 1B cap. **None of the three figures is the LA Times "$5.1 million"** (row 57 stands).

**Caution: two different "65 percent" figures.** The archive also uses "65 percent" for CPU utilization. The 65/35 cost split is a different quantity, and any sentence that uses both needs a unit.

### 1.2 The capacity benchmark kept the 1988 caseload by agreement (Wewerka letter, pp. 50–51)

Joe Wewerka (EDS) wrote to Judy E. Mitchell (HRS FLORIDA Project Director) on October 4, 1991, under REF E1794, subject "Capacity Benchmark Parameter Agreements." The letter records:

- "The actual per-case daily ongoing activity transaction rates experienced in cutover districts will form the basis for the benchmark daily transaction workload. … The benchmark daily transaction workload will be determined by multiplying the actual per-case transaction rates by the **RFP 1993+20% caseload volumes** for PA and CSE." (p. 50)
- Worker activities stand as transmitted in letters E1115 (EDS, March 28, 1991) and H1246 (August 23, 1991). The page image reads "H1246" (OCR "51246"); the H prefix suggests an HRS letter, which is inference. Occurrence factors approximate actual volumes, and primary activities are selected by an "80/20 cutoff for transaction volumes." (p. 50)
- For non-production activity, "RFP parameters will form the basis for any volume-related data," then the EDS proposal, then actual usage. (p. 50)
- "HRS and EDS agree that the RFP response time categories do not relate to the multiple-category capabilities of the FLORIDA system within a single interactive response. The parties agree to measure FLORIDA system response time using a more appropriate methodology, and will work together in good faith to develop a systematic basis for determining the standard(s) for that measurement." (p. 51)

**Why this matters to the archive's central question.** *Agent analysis, for Kastner to accept, revise or reject.* In October 1991 the parties corrected one input of the workload model, the per-case transaction rate, using measured production data. They also agreed in writing to keep the other input, the caseload, at the 1988 projection. By then the actual caseload had overrun that projection: 12061 ¶54 gives 318,655 Food Stamp cases as the FY 1992–93 design point against 547,159 actual in FY 1991–92, and EDS alleged the same in general terms (¶15, ¶20). The acceptance test was therefore defined to answer "does the machine carry the contract's workload?" and not "does it carry the State's workload?" This is the clearest documentary instance in the archive of a workload assumption outliving the model built on it (project concept `capacity-planning-assumptions`). It also helps explain the MOU's odd ownership clause (1A): HRS was to own the hardware even if the test showed it exceeded the RFP. Against a frozen 1988 caseload, that outcome was plausible on paper while the system was saturated in production.

**Bearing on row 36c.** Kastner's synthesis puts EDS's internal discovery that usable capacity was half of expectations in March 1991 (press-sourced). EDS letter E1115 is dated **March 28, 1991**, and it transmitted the PA and CSE worker-activity definitions used for the benchmark. *Inference only:* E1115 is a candidate contemporaneous record of the re-measured transaction model. It is a new records target and is not evidence of what EDS knew.

### 1.3 First verbatim text of the RFP performance standards (Exhibit E, pp. 70–79†)

Exhibit E reproduces RFP 88-74-BC pages VII-28 to VII-36: § 7.30 (warranty) and §§ 7.31–7.32 (liquidated damages). Until now the archive has quoted the response-time standard only through 12061 ¶51's paraphrase ("one to eight seconds … 95 percent"). The flag recorded in § 5 for the September 6 sweep was: "the RFP text itself remains unrecovered. Any future claim quoting the contract verbatim needs the RFP." For §§ 7.30–7.32 that is no longer true.

**§ 7.32.4 Response Time (pp. 75–76†), as far as the two-column OCR allows.**
- Measured during normal working hours, 7:00 AM to 6:30 PM Eastern, Monday through Friday, excluding state holidays. The OCR read "6:39"; the page image and the verification run read 6:30.
- Each standard is "measured every 15 minutes during a randomly selected day each month at randomly selected workstations."

| Category (names partly reconstructed †) | Standard |
|---|---|
| Client record search | 6 seconds, 95 percent of searches |
| Client record retrieval | 4 seconds, 95 percent of records retrieved |
| Edit | 3 seconds, 95 percent of the time |
| Screen-to-screen | 1 second, 95 percent of the time |
| Case assessment (computed data elements) | 8 seconds, 95 percent of the time |
| Print initiation | 3 seconds, 95 percent of the time |

- Liquidated damages follow for each response-time standard not met.
- Availability is 97 percent (§ 7.32.5), and workstation availability is 97 percent for each workstation (§ 7.32.6). The complaint lists the per-standard amounts as $15,000 per standard per month, $150 per workstation, and $500 per day for documentation. **All values must be verified against the page images before they are quoted.**

This confirms the 12061 paraphrase's range of one to eight seconds. It also shows what Wewerka's letter set aside: six **single-function** categories. The FLORIDA system's interactive transactions spanned several functions in one response.

### 1.7 EDS's allegations about the upgrade and IBM (allegations only)

- Before the MOU, EDS proposed a benchmark first to size the configuration. HRS refused and "directed EDS to install an $11.6 million computer upgrade on or before November 4, 1991" (¶¶17–18).
- "Separate and secret discussions between HRS and IBM precursed the MOU. … Upon information and belief, the $11.6 million upgrade was agreed upon in the secret discussions between HRS personnel, including Ms. Viann Hardy, and IBM personnel, without EDS's input or consent." (¶19)
- After June 1992, HRS replaced the contract equipment with upgraded equipment "apparently … acquired directly from IBM without … competitive bidding," and "upon information and belief" installed it only after IBM benchmark testing "(referred to as a 'snap-shot')" (¶48). This is EDS's account of the same 1992 IBM Snapshot the presentment describes (¶¶53–55). Row 60's guard applies: it has nothing to do with the 1988–89 SNAP/SHOT proposal modeling.
- The CPC equipment was installed in **October 1989** and "later upgraded pursuant to Contract Amendment 3 in **December 1990**" (¶79). This hardware increment is **not in the archive's capacity timeline**; its model is unstated.
- EDS alleged it "completed a benchmark test demonstrating satisfactory response time in March 1992," with results forwarded April 3, 1992 (¶79). The archive (main report Appendix A, from 12061) says no test was conducted "except for data submitted in April 1992." These agree on the April transmittal and differ on whether it was a test. Carry both and attribute both.
- HRS's June 1, 1992 "warranty claim" demanded tuning beyond the contract response times and work on ABENDs (¶¶41–42). HRS secretly declared an "emergency" and used contractors who had not bid (¶45). EDS offered free help at a July 21, 1992 meeting and was rejected (¶46).
- EDS stated it had paid more than $14.6 million for the central computer (¶93).
