### Why the letter is built this way

**It opens by conceding the office was responsive.** It was — fourteen days, no fee, four reports. Nothing is gained by treating the omission as a refusal when no refusal has been stated.

**Every item carries a number.** The office's own [report request page](https://flauditor.gov/pages/report_request.html) asks for name, mailing address, and report number, and treats the transaction as a copy order. That is very likely why the July delivery matched the four numbers in the closing sentence and missed the two described in prose. A request to this office should look like an order form.

**Item 5 is the one that changes the trajectory.** Confirmed by direct retrieval on August 21, 2026: no PDF exists at the expected path for 12061, 12581, 13043, 13256, or 13287, and the [archived report listings](https://flauditor.gov/pages/archived_reports.html) state that online coverage runs from July 1, 1995. Everything before that date is invisible from outside the office. Asking for the index rather than the documents is cheaper for the office to answer, and it converts every future ask from a description into a number.

**Item 6 corrects an error in the July letter.** That letter requested "the audit workpapers, or an index of the workpaper file, for Report No. 12581." Those are not obtainable under Chapter 119. Section 11.45(4)(c) provides:

> "The audit report when final shall be a public record. The audit workpapers and notes are not a public record; however, those workpapers necessary to support the computations in the final audit report may be made available by a majority vote of the Legislative Auditing Committee after a public hearing showing proper cause. The audit workpapers and notes shall be retained by the Auditor General until no longer useful in his or her proper functions, after which time they may be destroyed." ([s. 11.45, Fla. Stat.](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/statutes/2025/11.45))

Two things follow. The workpapers are a petition to the Joint Legislative Auditing Committee, not a records request, and no such petition should be filed before their survival is established. And the retention rule is a usefulness test with no fixed period, which means a 1995 workpaper file could equally have been kept or destroyed. Existence is not itself confidential, so the question in item 6 is answerable by a clerk in a minute. A negative closes the archive's highest-value technical line permanently, which is worth more than leaving it open.

**What the workpapers would be worth if they survive.** Report 12581 is the sole documentary source for the 95–100 percent CPU utilization finding, which the archive currently carries on press coverage alone. Its workpapers would hold the measurement data behind that figure — the closest thing to an instrumented record of how the machine actually performed.

### 5a. Response, August 19, 2026 — DCF reference P257883-072926

The Department of Children and Families acknowledged receipt on July 29, 2026, recited the request verbatim, and answered in a single sentence:

> The department has located no records which are responsive to your request.

No statutory basis is cited, no exemption is claimed, and no partial production is offered. Turnaround was roughly three weeks, which is prompt by Chapter 119 standards.

**What the answer is, and what it is not.** It is not a denial. A denial under s. 119.07(1)(d), Fla. Stat., must be stated in writing with the statutory citation; a finding of no responsive records escapes that requirement entirely, because on its face nothing has been withheld. The letter accordingly does not say whether the records once existed, whether they were destroyed, when, under what authority, where the Department looked, or whether the series was transferred elsewhere. All five requested items — the two-volume RFP, the Evaluation Manual and scoring instruments, the executed EDS contract, the Webster special-master report, and the 1996–97 settlement — return the same null.

**Section 5 predicted this, and the prediction was right.** The status note above, written July 28, flagged this as the request most likely to find the file destroyed under a retention schedule, and said the finding was worth having early. It is now had. The [General Records Schedule GS1-SL](https://admfin.ucf.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/GS1-SL-2017table.pdf) sets non-capital-improvement bid records — the series that would contain an RFP for an information system — at **five fiscal years after award**. RFP 88-74-BC was awarded in 1989. The file has been eligible for lawful destruction since roughly 1994, which is before the Webster arbitration and before the settlement. Nothing in the answer is surprising and nothing in it suggests bad faith.

**But a null return is not the same as a documented destruction, and Florida law supplies the difference.** Rule 1B-24.003(9)(d), F.A.C., requires an agency to document each disposition with the schedule number, item number, record series title, inclusive dates, volume, disposition action, and disposition date ([Managing Florida's Public Records](https://files.floridados.gov/media/704747/managing-floridas-public-records-2021.pdf)). That documentation is itself a public record, and it is not covered by the request as written. It is the correct next ask: DCF may hold no RFP, but if the RFP was destroyed it should hold the paper recording that the RFP was destroyed, when, and under which item — which converts an absence into a dated, citable fact.

**A second possibility the letter does not exclude.** Under s. 257.36(2)(a), Fla. Stat., records appraised as having historical value are transferred to the Florida State Archives, and custody moves with them even though title stays with the agency ([§ 257.36](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/257.36)). A departmental search returning nothing is fully consistent with the 1988 procurement series sitting in the R.A. Gray Building under an HRS accession.
