# Making It Workable: Remediation Measures on the FLORIDA System, 1991–1997

*A cost-and-effect table for the Kastner research archive*

**Compiled July 28, 2026** · Companion to "The Unisys Protests of RFP 88-74-BC"

---

## Note on the evidence

The FLORIDA system's remediation record is preserved in fragments. The two Auditor General reports that documented it in detail — **No. 12581** (audit period Oct. 17, 1994–Feb. 28, 1995, issued Sept. 12, 1995) and **No. 13043**, "HRS Settlement of FLORIDA Contract with EDS" (period July 1, 1995–Dec. 31, 1996, issued Aug. 25, 1997) — predate the Auditor General's online-PDF retention cutoff and are available only by mail request ([FY1995-96 listing](https://flauditor.gov/pages/list9596page.htm); [FY1997-98 listing](https://flauditor.gov/pages/list9798page.htm)). Report 12581's substance survives almost entirely through the [St. Petersburg Times account of September 15, 1995](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1995/09/15/hrs-supercomputer-can-t-hack-it-report-says/), published the day after issuance. The table below therefore separates what is documented from what is known to exist but not yet recovered. The outstanding records requests already drafted in this archive would close most of the gaps.

## What the table shows

**Total identified capital remediation: $10.5 million.** Two CPU upgrades against a program that had consumed roughly $200 million by 1995 and generated a further $42 million judgment. GAO's [AIMD-94-52FS](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-94-52FS/html/GAOREPORTS-AIMD-94-52FS.htm) projected a $5.6 million Florida mainframe upgrade for the FY1993–94 period, almost certainly the $5.4 million purchase that materialized. The hardware bill was small because hardware was never the binding constraint.

**The cheapest intervention was the most effective.** Moving staff to nights and Saturdays cut response time in half, from eight seconds to four. The $5.4 million CPU upgrade delivered 20 percent. Rescheduling human beings outperformed silicon by a wide margin — the signature of a workload whose peak concurrency, not whose aggregate throughput, had been mis-modeled. Spreading the same transactions across more hours works when the problem is a peak; buying more processor works when the problem is volume. That the former worked better is direct evidence about the shape of the defect.

**The measures were overwhelmingly demand-side.** Of the fifteen entries, only two are capacity purchases. The rest ration, defer, or redistribute the load: limiting concurrent users, extending office hours, moving work to nights and weekends, deferring recertifications, and — in the 1991–92 conversion — simply not performing eligibility reviews at all and letting benefits auto-extend. That last workaround produced 399,320 cases USDA required Florida to re-examine, and contributed to the $5 million food-stamp penalty and the $260 million in cumulative errors reported by 1993.

**The costs were shifted onto people who could not refuse them.** Extended staff hours, Saturday shifts, and concurrent-user rationing appear in the record with no dollar figure attached, because they were not paid for in appropriations. They were paid for in caseworker overtime and displaced schedules, and in client waiting — the "long lines for benefits" Webster's order describes. The true remediation cost of the FLORIDA system is therefore substantially larger than any figure in Table 1, and structurally invisible in the accounting.

**A system running at 95–100 percent CPU was contractually compliant.** The 60–65 percent utilization rule the same hearing officer had recited as a finding of fact in [88-2942BID ¶56](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1988/88002942.PDF) nine months before award was a design convention, not a term. Webster could find the system compliant because nothing in the contract, in FAMIS, or in [45 CFR §205.37](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/205.37) obliged it to have headroom. Every measure in this table exists because there was no standard requiring that they not be necessary.

**The remediation destroyed its own maintenance capacity.** In March 1995, at the point of maximum stress, HRS terminated Deloitte & Touche and lost roughly 60 percent of the programming staff overnight. The emergency award went to Unisys — the vendor that had protested the original procurement in 1988 and testified that the response times would not be met.

| Missing item | Where it likely exists |
|---|---|
| Dollar value of the extended-hours and Saturday-shift program (overtime, differentials) | HRS budget amendments and Auditor General **Report 12581** workpapers |
