# One Model, Forty Years: Lessons Learned and Scored Prescience from the FLORIDA System, 1986–2028

**Peter S. Kastner**
*Kastner research archive · July 28, 2026*

---

## Bottom line up front

In March 1989 a Florida administrative hearing officer wrote down the correct principle of capacity modeling and then ruled against it in the same paragraph. He observed that "the data generated by the IBM modeling programs are no better than the assumptions that went into the models," acknowledged that the state's own evaluators' "understanding of the models, assumptions, and even the resulting data was incomplete," and concluded anyway that "the capacity-planning assumptions are reasonable" ([DOAH 89-0003BID](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1989/89000003.PDF), Appendix ¶90). Six years later a special master found that Electronic Data Systems had known since March 1991 that those assumptions overstated usable capacity by roughly a factor of two, had not told the state, and that "good faith and fair dealing required EDS to disclose the erroneous modeling assumption" ([St. Petersburg Times, Aug. 16, 1995](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1995/08/16/faulty-computer-could-cost-state-more/)).

I was the witness whose testimony was discounted in that appendix. I say that at the top rather than at the bottom, because everything that follows is an attempt to score a record I am inside of, and the reader is entitled to weigh the source before reading the scores.

Here is my conclusion after assembling this archive. **The FLORIDA system did not fail because anyone chose a bad tool, a bad vendor, or a bad architecture. It failed because no institution in the chain — not the state, not the tribunal, not HHS, not USDA — had any mechanism for testing a vendor's assumptions about the vendor's own workload.** Everyone tested the model. Nobody tested the inputs. That gap was identified by the Government Accountability Office in 1981, seven years before Florida issued the RFP ([GAO/HRD-81-119](https://www.gao.gov/products/hrd-81-119)); it was still open when the state signed the contract in 1989; and outside of one program it is still open in 2026.

The system procured in 1988 is still the production eligibility engine of the State of Florida. It is a "legacy mainframe system more than thirty years old," and the project to replace it "is anticipated to be completed in 2028 and cost an estimated $183 Million" ([Fla. Senate SB 1758 bill analysis, Jan. 30, 2026](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2026/1758/Analyses/2026s01758.pre.hp.PDF), quoting *Chianne D. v. Harris*, No. 3:23-cv-985-MMH-LLL, 2026 WL 32126, at *13, *16 (M.D. Fla. Jan. 6, 2026)). That is the far end of the arc: forty years from Advance Planning Document to retirement, if 2028 holds. I do not believe 2028 holds, and I score that prediction below along with everyone else's.

## 2. What the record actually establishes

Five findings carry the analytical weight. I restate them here in compressed form because the scoring in Sections 4 through 8 depends on them.

**First, the modeling was IBM's, performed for EDS.** The tribunal called it "the elaborate computer modeling conducted by EDS and IBM" ([89-0003BID](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1989/89000003.PDF) ¶145) and credited "the informed use of modeling by IBM" (Appendix ¶90). The vendor whose hardware would be bought produced the study that determined how much hardware would be needed. Nobody in the record treats that as a conflict.

**Second, the tool was sound.** SNAP/SHOT was a legitimate discrete-event simulator with a published methodology. I have found no public record of a defect in it, and I am not asserting one.

**Third, the code was real and countable.** The transferred Ohio CRIS-E code existed. Instruction path lengths and disk I/O counts per transaction type were therefore knowable by inspection.

**Fourth, the failure lived in the two inputs no code inspection produces**: the transaction mix (`MSGINMIX`) and the arrival rate (`MSGRATE`). Those are behavioral parameters. They describe what caseworkers will actually do, at what hours, in what proportions. No amount of examining the donor system's source code tells you either one.

**Fifth — and this is the structural finding — the procurement contained no mechanism for testing a vendor's assumptions about its own workload.** Worse, ¶146 of the award order ranked "extrapolations from existing case loads" *beneath* simulation modeling as a capacity-estimation method. Extrapolation from the existing caseload is the only honest source of a transaction-mix estimate. The tribunal correctly named the right input, correctly identified its only source, and expressly called it inferior. Read this finding narrowly, because an earlier version of this archive read it too broadly: the contract did carry quantified performance standards and a benchmark-and-capacity-test mechanism for measuring them after delivery (Auditor General Report No. 12061, ¶¶49–59). What the procurement had no mechanism for was testing the vendor's workload assumptions *before* award, when the sizing was still changeable.

**A correction of terminology, stated for the record.** Throughout this archive I say "capacity assumptions were wrong," not "the capacity model was wrong." The distinction is not pedantry. The model was arithmetic performed correctly on numbers that were false. Webster's own phrase — "the erroneous modeling assumption," singular — is the right one and is preserved verbatim wherever it appears here.

## 3. Lessons learned

These are stated prescriptively, in the order I would give them to a state CIO facing a large benefits procurement in 2026. Every one of them is derived from the record above, and each carries the pinpoint that supports it.

**Lesson 1 — Buy the assumptions, not the model.** Any capacity study submitted with a bid should be accompanied by a machine-readable parameter file and a written derivation for every behavioral input. If the vendor cannot say where `MSGINMIX` came from, the vendor does not know what it is selling. The 1989 tribunal reviewed the sophistication of the method and never examined the inputs; that is the whole disaster in one sentence.

**Lesson 2 — Do not let the hardware vendor size the hardware.** IBM modeled the configuration IBM would sell. The record contains no independent capacity review of any kind. In my experience across four decades of enterprise procurement, the vendor's own sizing study is a sales instrument that has been formatted to look like an engineering document. Read it as the former.

**Lesson 3 — Extrapolation from the live caseload is evidence, not weakness.** ¶146 held the opposite. The measured behavior of 1.5 million existing cases is the only empirical thing in the room. A Monte Carlo sweep over invented parameters is a beautifully packaged guess. Prefer the messy real number to the elegant synthetic one — always, and especially when the elegant one comes from the seller.

**Lesson 4 — Liquidated damages do not create truthful assumptions; they create litigation.** The tribunal reasoned that the "motivation to develop accurate assumptions is strong due to the severe provisions for damages in the fixed-price contract" (¶145). What actually happened: EDS terminated on May 31, 1992, sued the state for $46 million on August 21, and was awarded $42 million plus interest in 1995 ([631 So. 2d 353](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1895493/state-dhrs-v-eds-federal-corp/); [St. Petersburg Times](https://www.tampabay.com/archive/1995/08/16/faulty-computer-could-cost-state-more/)). The $25 million performance bond and the damages clauses did not merely fail to deter; they became the mechanism by which the vendor extracted money from the state. Note that the *same hearing officer*, eight months earlier in the specifications protest, had written that the contractual "carrot and stick" approach "cannot enable the contractor to do the impossible" and instead "raises the likelihood of litigation" ([88-2942BID](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1988/88002942.PDF), Appendix ¶77–78). He was right in August 1988 and forgot it by March 1989.

**Lesson 5 — An unimplemented transfer base is not a transfer base.** The winning proposal was built on Ohio's CRIS-E, "which has never been implemented, does not serve any clients or cases, and features centralized architecture rather than the distributed architecture sought by HRS" (¶134). The losing proposal offered New York systems that "have been implemented and certified… feature distributed architecture" (¶135). An evaluator recorded that for EDS "all proven components will be used" — factually wrong, since CRIS-E "was still in acceptance testing and had never been proven by actual use in the field" (¶143). Require production evidence: transaction volumes, elapsed months in live service, named reference sites.

**Lesson 6 — Watch what the architecture requirement does between the APD and the RFP.** Florida's Advance Planning Document *required* distributed processing. By the time RFP 88-74-BC hit the street, at FNS's direction, it was a *preference* (¶26). A centralized design then won on a capacity study, and the resulting single host is the thing that ran out of headroom in 1991, could not be relieved except by rationing users, was declared end-of-life in 2012, and is still being migrated in 2026. One word changed in one document in 1988 and the consequence is still on the state's books thirty-eight years later.

**Lesson 7 — Utilization headroom was the one performance number left out of the contract, and leaving it out was a defect.** The 60–65 percent utilization convention appears in the record as expert testimony, not as an enforceable obligation ([88-2942BID](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1988/88002942.PDF) ¶56). Response time and availability were written into the contract as numbers; utilization headroom was not. And utilization is where the machine's condition shows first: an October 11, 1994 report by an independent consultant hired by the Department found regular-day CPU utilization at 95 percent and peak-period at 100 percent; the 65 percent comparison is industry data the Department obtained, not an Auditor General measurement (Auditor General Report No. 12581, ¶44). The Auditor General reported both figures without independent verification and appended the caution that "estimates for practical maximum CPU capacity may vary." Department records separately show the ES9021/900 running at approximately 95 percent during regular workday hours (Report No. 12581, p. 29). Put the utilization number in the contract alongside the response-time and availability numbers, and make the buyer, not the seller, choose who measures it.

**Lesson 8 — A quantified standard with no running acceptance mechanism is not a standard.** **The archive was wrong about this lesson, and the error was mine.** It previously read "absence of a numeric requirement guarantees an unfalsifiable dispute," inferring from the appellate record's silence that RFP 88-74-BC specified no numeric throughput, response-time, or availability requirement. The correct reading is that the contract carried a two-part quantified performance standard: response times of 1 to 8 seconds at least 95 percent of the time, sampled every 15 minutes at a random day and workstation on a monthly basis, and availability of at least 97 percent over rolling 30-day periods (Auditor General Report No. 12061, ¶¶49–59). Tina Greene made the audit measurements under Jonathan Ingram's supervision for Auditor General Charles L. Lester: availability of 94 percent for the period ending March 29, 1992, 80.5 percent for the period ending April 28, 1992, and 97.9 percent for the period ending May 28, 1992 — the first month the standard was met — and a manual response-time test on May 13, 1992 averaging 2.8 minutes against a specification of 1 to 8 seconds. The standard existed and was missed by two orders of magnitude. What did not exist was an enforcement path. The Department's own response to Finding #5 states that "EDS postponed and rescheduled these deliverables, thereby delaying the tests, until they were useless, since the system had already experienced performance problems and system unavailability," and an October 4, 1991 Memorandum of Understanding modified the contract's benchmark obligations and waived liquidated damages for the delay in the benchmark tests. Write the number, then protect the test that measures it: a standard whose acceptance test can be rescheduled by the party being tested is a standard the seller controls.

**Lesson 8a — When the requirement is qualitative, no post-hoc measurement can prove a breach.** This is what survives of the old Lesson 8, and it applies to the procurement phase rather than to the contract. The tribunal found "no credible evidence that the system proposed by EDS fails to satisfy any response times in the RFP" (¶73) while the only response-time language quoted anywhere in the protest record is on-line access "in a matter of seconds" (¶36 of [88-2942BID](https://www.doah.state.fl.us/ROS/1988/88002942.PDF)). The numbers were in the procurement — Report 12061 proves it — and the tribunal that ruled on whether they would be met never put them on the record. A bid protest decided against a qualitative paraphrase of a quantified requirement is decided on the wrong document.

**Lesson 9 — Remediation buys seconds, not architecture.** Response time went from minutes in June 1992 to 3 seconds in December 1994 by way of two CPU upgrades ($5.1 million c. 1992–93 and $5.4 million in April 1995, the latter yielding roughly 20 percent), extended staff hours, and active rationing of concurrent users. None of that changed the topology. Thirty-two years later the state is still paying to undo the topology.

**Lesson 10 — The federal government's function in this chain was fiscal, not engineering, and it still is.** Federal approval "covered cost allocation and scope, not source-selection methodology." There was "no federal capacity standard to invoke, no federal technical reviewer to defer to, no certification test to await." A state could be certified under 45 CFR §307.10 with a system running at 100 percent CPU and unusable response times: certification and usability were orthogonal. When GAO recommended in 1992 that HHS and USDA stand up a joint program office with on-site inspection authority, **both agencies refused** ([GAO/IMTEC-92-29](https://www.gao.gov/products/imtec-92-29)).

**Lesson 11 — When the fix finally arrives, it arrives for one program, decades late.** The FNS rewrite of [7 CFR §277.18](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-7/subtitle-B/chapter-II/subchapter-C/part-277/section-277.18), effective January 2, 2014, is substantively the rule whose absence defined this disaster: mandatory acceptance testing including "stress and throughput performance testing," verification against "performance standards including responsiveness, usability, capacity and security," and a live pilot of normally three months or more before statewide rollout, with FNS concurrence a condition of continued federal funding. It arrived twenty-five and a half years after RFP 88-74-BC, and it governs SNAP. Medicaid's nearest analogue, [42 CFR §433.112(b)(17)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-C/part-433/subpart-C), still contains no number.

**Lesson 12 — Independent verification triggered by distress is not oversight.** Under the 2010 APD rewrite, IV&V "may be required" only once a project already shows one of eight risk indicators — missed deadlines, cost overrun, at risk of failure ([45 CFR Part 95 Subpart F](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-95/subpart-F)). It is a distress signal, not a gate, and the verifier is retained by the state being verified. A gate you only reach after you have already fallen is not a gate.

## 4. Scoring method

Each forward-looking claim below is rated **high**, **medium**, **low**, or **not-applicable** for prescience, following this archive's ingest vocabulary. A rating of *high* means the claim was substantially borne out by the documented record; *low* means it was falsified; *medium* means it was directionally right and materially wrong in magnitude, timing, or mechanism; *not-applicable* means the record does not permit scoring, and I say why.

I am scoring myself in Section 7. The reader should discount accordingly, and a subsequent pass will re-score all of this independently.

**What the federal column shows.** The April 1988 OTA staff were good forecasters about *what would be built* and poor forecasters about *what technology would build it*. The oversight community — GAO in 1981 and 1992, the California auditor in 1995, DOL OIG and GAO in 2023 — has been right, repeatedly, in writing, for forty-five years, about the same missing thing. And the institutional answer has been the same each time. I conclude that the absence is not an oversight. It is a preference. Federal agencies do not want inspection authority over state systems because inspection authority creates federal liability for state failures, and the current arrangement leaves that liability with the state.

## 10. Conclusions

**One.** The FLORIDA system is not a story about a bad computer. It is a story about a procurement that had no way to test a number. The number was the transaction mix, the mix was invented, the invention was defensible only because nobody had standing to ask where it came from, and thirty-seven years later no rule outside SNAP requires anyone to ask.

**Two.** Every party that got it right in 1988 was overruled, and the record shows they were overruled for reasons of method rather than of engineering. Simulation beat extrapolation. Sophistication beat measurement. A fixed-price contract with damages clauses was accepted as a proxy for vendor honesty, and it turned out to be a proxy for vendor leverage.

**Three.** The federal government's role in the 1986–1999 period was to pay for systems and to verify that the paying was correctly allocated. It was not to verify that the systems worked, and it did not. When GAO proposed changing that in 1992, HHS and USDA declined. The same proposal, to a different department, drew a partial concurrence in 2023. Make no mistake: this is a settled institutional preference, not a series of accidents.

**Four.** The one real fix — FNS's 2014 rewrite of 7 CFR §277.18 — is a good rule. It requires stress and throughput testing, verification against capacity and responsiveness standards, and a live pilot before statewide rollout, with continued federal funding conditioned on agency concurrence. Any state CIO reading this should treat that rule as the template regardless of program, because in Medicaid, child welfare, and unemployment insurance nothing equivalent exists.

**Five.** The system bought in 1989 for $107.7 million, delivered at a documented floor of $310.6 million through fiscal year 1996–97 on the Department's own accounting (Auditor General Report No. 13287, p. 14) — an overrun of about 188 percent, not the 128 percent this archive carried until this correction — declared end-of-life in 2012, is still determining who eats in Florida in 2026. The state's own plan puts the end of the migration in 2028 at a further $183 million. I believe that date will slip. I have been wrong before in this record — the tribunal said so in 1989 — but I would rather be scored than be safe.
