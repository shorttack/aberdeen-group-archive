# The Managed Conversation: Analyst Relations and Executive Access in the Golden Age of IT
*Aberdeen Group Memoirs, Volume 2 — Chapter 1*

***
## Setting the Table
There is a lunch that Aberdeen's training staff still remember. John Logan and I would take a group of newly hired analysts — mostly Gen X, mostly brilliant, mostly unfinished — to Maison Robert in Boston, one of the city's finest French restaurants. The stated purpose was professional development. The actual experience was closer to hazing.

Someone would invariably put their roll on the wrong plate. Someone else would hesitate when asked to choose the wine. A third would address the waiter incorrectly or fail to read the table's social hierarchy before speaking. Logan and I would pounce, quietly but without mercy. The kitchen staff named the recurring affair "the throw up lunch," and they were not wrong about the anxiety level.

But here is what the name missed: the lunch was not punishment. It was inoculation. By the early 2000s, Aberdeen's analysts were expected to perform at the level of a Fortune 500 executive's dinner table, because that is exactly where they would find themselves — seated next to an IBM general manager, or across from a senior vice president at a vendor who was deciding whether you were worth listening to, or attending a client dinner where your composure and social fluency were being evaluated as carefully as your research. The wine selection protocol alone carried signal: the analyst is typically asked to choose, is nominally on the vendor's expense account, and should demonstrate knowledge and restraint by selecting something credible but not extravagant — a Loire Valley white, perhaps, or a modest Burgundy. Choosing the most expensive bottle signals either ignorance or indifference to the vendor-analyst relationship. Choosing the cheapest signals insecurity. The choice is a small performance of judgment.

Analyst relations — the function, the profession, and the politics of it — operated the same way. Every interaction was a performance, and the stakes were higher than a dinner tab.

## The Japanese Tea Service
To understand what analyst relations had become by the late 1990s, imagine the Japanese tea ceremony. Every movement is choreographed. The host has thought through the guest's experience in advance. The utensils are positioned with deliberate intent. Nothing is improvised. Nothing is casual. The experience is beautiful and almost entirely controlled.

The formal vendor briefing, by that era, was the analytical equivalent. A major vendor — IBM, Oracle, Microsoft, Intel, Cisco — would invite a senior analyst to a scheduled briefing. An executive would present. An AR minder would sit to the side, ostensibly taking notes but in practice monitoring every word exchanged. The executive's remarks were prepared. The supporting materials were vetted. The follow-up process was defined. Even the informal "chat" after the formal presentation had been thought about in advance.

This was not paranoia; it was professionalism, vendor-style. AR had learned, across a decade of hard experience, that an offhand remark from an executive could become the headline of an analyst memoir, that a candid admission about a product delay could ripple through a customer base, and that an analyst who left a briefing with the wrong impression — or the right one, expressed prematurely — could cost a vendor millions in pipeline, not to mention stock option losses. The tea ceremony was a response to real risk.

The problem was what it displaced. The genuinely useful analyst-executive conversation — the back-and-forth, the challenge and counter-challenge, the moment where an executive's assumption gets stress-tested by someone who has spent months studying the competitive landscape — that conversation could not survive the ceremony. The AR apparatus was too good at its job.

## The Three-Inch Folder
The sophistication of a mature AR operation becomes vivid in a single memory. In the late 1990s, there was a one-on-one briefing with John M. Thompson, then general manager heading IBM's Software Group. The conversation was good — the kind of genuine exchange that becomes possible when trust has been established over years of professional interaction. It ranged, as such conversations do, into adjacent territory. A passing remark about a side technology became, in the moment, a casual statement of opinion.

The AR minder in the room — who had been silent throughout — immediately spoke for the first time. "Oh, no, Peter, that's not what you wrote."

Before there was time to respond, she had reached into a lawyer's valise at her feet, extracted a folder three inches thick, and opened it to a specific press clipping in seconds: a quoted Kastner remark from an earlier published piece. She read it aloud as evidence.

The semantic difference was genuine and easily explained. The briefing continued. But something had just become clear that had not been fully understood before: IBM had catalogued everything said in public, and had assigned a trained professional whose job included knowing that catalogue by heart. This was not a secretary with a filing cabinet; this was an intelligence operation. The folder at her feet was not administrative overhead; it was a weapon held in reserve, deployed at the precise moment it was needed.

The reaction was pride, and something close to alarm. Pride because the existence of that folder meant IBM considered the research consequential enough to track. Alarm because the full implications were suddenly visible: every published word, every conference remark, every quoted comment in a trade publication was on record, indexed, and could be surfaced against you in a formal briefing at any time.

That folder would be worth something today as a record of thirty years of published thinking. At the time, it was a reminder that by the late 1990s, analyst relations at the major vendors was no longer a scheduling function. It was risk management, narrative control, and competitive intelligence in a single discipline.

## AR as Gatekeeper
The structural power of AR derived from a simple fact: AR controlled access. Access to executives, access to roadmaps, access to beta programs, access to customer references — all of it flowed through AR, and AR could adjust the valve at will.

A vendor with a sophisticated AR operation could quietly exclude an analyst from early briefings on a new product, ensuring that by the time the analyst wrote about it, analyst-competitors were already positioned in the market. AR could steer preferred analysts toward favorable customer references while steering independent-minded analysts toward reference customers who had been coached on messaging. AR could grant or withhold the executive meeting that elevated a briefing from transactional to strategic. None of this required formal policy or explicit instruction. It happened through the thousand small choices that an AR professional made every day about whose calls to return first, whose requests to expedite, whose briefing request to schedule in the prime slot versus the last hour of the day.

For analyst firms that cultivated their AR relationships, this system worked reasonably well. Vendors felt managed, analysts felt informed, and the commercial arrangements that increasingly underpinned the industry — contracts, advisory retainers, event sponsorships — were smoothed along by functional AR relationships.

For Aberdeen, which came of professional age in an earlier era and never fully recalibrated to AR's new power, the system created an invisible tax. The firm's research quality was not in question, but its willingness to coddle AR, to participate in the relationship maintenance that AR increasingly demanded, was inconsistent. In that vacuum, others could operate. Vendors with grievances about Aberdeen's coverage had a natural ally in their own AR teams, and AR consultants who offered "competitive" services — essentially, helping vendors manage or neutralize analyst coverage they found inconvenient — found a receptive audience.

The irony is that Aberdeen's foundational value — independent, evidence-based research that told vendors things they needed to hear rather than things they wanted to hear — was precisely what made it valuable to executives while making it uncomfortable to AR. AR's job was to manage the narrative. Aberdeen's job was to challenge it. The two were structurally in tension, and Aberdeen, lacking a deliberate AR strategy, didn't always recognize that tension in time to manage it.

## When Executives Break Free
The system had a consistent vulnerability: executives who were actually accountable for strategy would, at moments of genuine uncertainty, break out of it.

The AS/400 case is instructive. An IBM hardware general manager, Bill Zeitler — carrying real responsibility for a business whose market position he did not fully trust — bypassed every formal channel and picked up the phone. Could John Logan and Peter Kastner come to Somers, New York for lunch that week? No fees, no formal engagement, no AR present. Just a private dining room, a business problem, and two analysts whose judgment he trusted.

At that lunch, the general manager said plainly that he did not trust the market position his AS/400 organization was giving him. What did we think?

This was not an unusual question. It was an unusual context: a senior IBM executive confessing internal doubt to outside analysts because going through the official system — engaging AR, commissioning a formal study, routing the question through IBM's own market research apparatus — would have taken months, cost hundreds of thousands of dollars, and produced an answer filtered through the very organizational consensus he was trying to interrogate. The lunch cost the price of an IBM internal lunch. The answer arrived the same afternoon.

The methodology was familiar from expert witness work: walk around the position, identify the elements that are defensible, then pull the supports out from underneath by tracing the long-term and subordinate implications of the assumptions. Not a frontal attack, but a structural one. The position collapsed under its own weight once those supports were named.

The general manager got what he needed: not permission to doubt, which he already had, but a rigorous external framework for understanding exactly where the doubt was justified. AR could never have participated in that conversation, because AR's function was to protect the organization's narrative, not to challenge it. The executive needed the challenge, not the protection.

Lew Platt, chairman and CEO of Hewlett-Packard, had the Aberdeen partners for private dinners in Palo Alto. There were other executives who broke free, but it was not common.

## The Ego Trip, Properly Understood
Not all access problems required a phone call. Some required architecture.

Aberdeen's conference operation in the Middle East, built by the firm's regional distributor Ali Kamali and based in Dubai, offered something the formal AR apparatus could not easily counter: a prestigious independent platform that gave executives a legitimate reason to participate on their own terms.

The mechanism was deliberate. Each year, the conference ended with a formal banquet where a senior technology figure received a lifetime achievement award and delivered a keynote. The first such honoree was Gene Amdahl — a genuine pioneer whose presence consecrated the platform as serious. The second year, the outreach went to Oracle's Larry Ellison.

Ellison had a conflict and could not attend in person. But he did something more valuable: he invited us to his home in Atherton — filled with Samurai antiques, a collector's house with an aesthetic the size of the ambition behind it — and gave a forty-five minute off-the-cuff interview conversation on camera, no edits -- with no AR present, no prepared remarks, no minder keeping notes. Just Ellison, a couch, a cameraman, me, and an open conversation about technology and the future.

The following year, Al Gore.

The pattern was not accidental. The platform worked as an "ego trip" in the most precise strategic sense: it gave executives something they genuinely valued — recognition from an independent authority, association with a prestigious international audience — in exchange for participation that bypassed their own AR gatekeeping. No executive needed to explain to AR why he was appearing at a prestigious international technology conference to receive a lifetime achievement award or deliver a keynote. The prestige of the platform was the mechanism. The candid conversation was the result.

This is the inverse of how AR normally operates. AR controls access *to* executives. The Dubai conference created a platform that executives wanted access *to*. Reversing the direction of desire reversed the power dynamic entirely.

## The Briefing That Said Everything
A briefing with Greg Joswiak — "Joz," Apple's head of product marketing, later Senior Vice President of Worldwide Marketing — brought these dynamics to a single sharp moment.

Apple by the mid-2000s had formalized its analyst tier system with characteristic precision. There would be no paid relationship with Aberdeen; that had been made unmistakably clear. The firm's access would be managed, structured, and bounded. The early Apple — the company that had given Aberdeen four Macintosh computers and a laser printer and paid to distribute research on client-server computing in 1991 — had been replaced by something far more controlled.

Joswiak came through Boston with the original iPod after a full day of analyst briefings. He was tired and behind schedule, running on the caffeine of someone who had explained the same product vision a dozen times before breakfast. When asked what drew me to the device, the answer was honest: it fit a specific music sensibility perfectly. The iPod was not a technology category; it was a music experience. For people who had spent decades with serious listening habits, the iPod was not a gadget but a restoration of something that had been lost in the CD era.

Joswiak turned to the AR people in the room and said, "Finally, someone who gets it."

The response was immediate: "Sorry, I'm not allowed to advise you."

His expression shifted. He understood precisely what had just been said — not the surface meaning, but the structural one. Here was an analyst who understood the product and market at the level where useful advice lived, and that analyst was telling him, as directly as courtesy allowed, that Joz's own AR apparatus had made it impossible for him to access that advice informally. The AR people in the room controlled the channel between them. He was an executive in his own company, and the channel was not his to manage.

He put me on the new iPod list for three years. Every subsequent meeting included AR. The conversation that might have been valuable never happened.

## The St. Martin Protocol
The most elegant end-run around AR constraints in this period came from Charles Wang, founder of Computer Associates and for several years Aberdeen's and my largest client.

Wang needed a day of unpaid consulting from five senior industry analysts to think through a strategic question — invest or cash-cow the Ingres database, which CA had acquired in 1994 when the asset was flagging. Unpaid consulting from senior analysts is, formally speaking, not allowed. The conflict-of-interest implications are obvious, and AR at most firms would have flagged the arrangement immediately.

Wang's solution was architectural. The venue was his home on St. Martin. Attendance included a plus-one for each analyst. The hotel was covered through Sunday. The Friday "meeting" was framed as a collegial gathering of industry thinkers at a private residence, not a vendor-managed consulting session. The distinction was fine enough to be technically defensible and obvious enough that everyone understood exactly what was happening.

The strategic question got its answer — cash cow — over a working lunch. But the real agenda revealed itself at that same lunch. Wang had deliberately seated his guest at his left, the position of honor, and leaned over during the meal to make a request: there was a customer problem at Prudential, and he understood from earlier professional history that there was a Kastner connection to Art Ryan, Prudential's CEO. Could he arrange a one-on-one introduction?

That, in retrospect, was the reason for the invitation to St. Martin. The strategic consulting question was genuine but secondary. The primary asset Wang needed was a relationship — a direct line to a CEO who could resolve a significant customer issue — and the only way to access that asset was through the kind of personal relationship that formal AR channels cannot create or manage.

The introduction was made by me. CA kept the Prudential account.

## The Early Days: Waggoner Edstrom and the Hood Canal
To understand how profoundly AR changed, it is worth remembering what it looked like before the apparatus was built.

In the early 1990s, Microsoft's analyst relations function was handled by an outside PR agency: Waggoner Edstrom. That single fact contains a complete picture. Microsoft had not yet understood — could not yet have understood, given where the company was in its evolution — that analyst relations was a strategic function requiring dedicated professionals, institutional memory, and a managed intelligence operation. It was yet another communications problem, so it was assigned to communications professionals.

The result was that a dozen analysts got on a seaplane in Seattle one mid-afternoon and flew west to Bill Gates' family summer home on the Hood Canal. Buffet dinner. No minders in the formal sense. Real access.

Later that evening, after the East Coast analysts had gone to bed, there was an extended conversation with Jim Allchin, who was then leading the Windows NT project. What Allchin described was extraordinary: a project the scale of the Apollo moon program, perhaps four thousand programmers writing in C with crude tools, no repository, a semi-GUI homemade coding desktop running on Intel 386 processors. The due date was indeterminate — years away, possibly. The methodology was brute force.

For someone with application generator experience at Stratus, where a dozen engineers had built a MULTICS-clone operating system in a single year using PL/1, or where I had built Chase manhattan's trust accounting system with one other programmer, the NT methodology prompted a visceral reaction. Frederick Brooks had written the essential truth about software development decades earlier in *The Mythical Man Month*: adding programmers to a late software project makes it later. Microsoft was solving an architectural problem with headcount, and Allchin seemed genuinely unable to see another way.

The mental image that formed that night — a leather-jacketed Genghis Khan leading a horde of programmers — eventually became the research memo "NT: The Emperor Has No Clothes," published in 1997. That memo could not have been written without the Hood Canal briefing. And the Hood Canal briefing happened because Microsoft's AR function was not yet sophisticated enough to prevent it.

Later that same evening, Bill Gates walked over to poke the fire. There was a scotch to refresh. We talked alone for 45 minutes.

Gates had received a death threat that morning. Someone had sent him a photograph taken through his car window, his head framed in a gunsight. He had no security detail until that day; one was being assembled urgently. His life — the open, accessible life of a wealthy technology founder who moved through Seattle without an entourage — was ending. He understood that as clearly as he had understood anything.

He needed someone to talk to. The conversation ranged widely. When Oracle came up as a competitor, he pushed back — "No, they're a good partner." My retort: "Well, coopetition." He was a man of famously strong strategic views, well-read and confident in his frameworks. But he did not see, standing by that fire, what Windows NT on x86 servers was about to become: one of the two dominant enterprise development platforms of the late 1990s and 2000's. He did not see that completing that platform would require a relational database alongside NT. He was solving a different problem, protecting the desktop market. He had no interest in the enterprise server market, which I saw as destiny.

The decision formed quickly: there would be no Kastner retainer with Microsoft. Not because of hostility, but because the advisory relationship would require educating Gates toward a strategic framework he was not yet ready to hear — and Gates, in that moment and on those terms, was not going to listen. Better to write the research clearly, publish the critique honestly, and leave the relationship at arm's length. From that point forward, Microsoft was, professionally speaking, "don't go there" territory. One peripheral product group, nothing more.

## The NCR Tour and the Analyst Who Was Enabled
The full picture of analyst relations requires its counterexample — the AR function working as it was meant to work, serving the analyst rather than managing him.

In the early 1990s, a speaking tour with NCR across Asia and Europe crystallized what good AR actually looked like. Mark Hurd — who would later run both HP and Oracle, but was then a younger executive at NCR with the instincts of someone who already understood how markets worked — and I built a joint program around Open OLTP: Unix OS on NCR 3000 multi-microprocessor servers, NCR's Top End transaction monitor running across multiple platforms, and a relational database. The theme came directly from the 1987 Aberdeen research memo "The Future of OLTP." The message was simple and genuinely true: buyers no longer had to pay the IBM mainframe tax, and they had real flexibility in how they scaled.

The tour covered Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, Malaysia, Hong Kong, Manila, Japan, and Korea. Attendance was large. Interest was genuine. The program succeeded.

The AR minder who traveled through every customs queue and every venue walk-through with that program was Tom Rampenthal — an Ohio part-time farmer who loved motorcycles and had no interest in the spotlight. Tom never gave a presentation. Tom never fact-checked a remark in real time. Tom made sure the logistics worked so that the analyst could arrive at each venue prepared, rested enough to be useful, and focused on the actual work. He turned a grueling itinerary through early-modern Asia into something that had moments of genuine wonder: the specific light over the harbor in Hong Kong, the particular quality of attention in a Singapore audience, the earthquake in Taiwan, the formality of Tokyo and the warmth beneath it.

That is AR in its original and best form: invisible support in the service of the analyst's work. Not management. Not control. Enablement.

## What Was Lost and What Remained
The evolution described across these stories follows a single arc. In the early 1990s, AR was a logistics function, sometimes outsourced entirely, and genuine access was possible because the apparatus to prevent it did not yet exist. By the late 1990s and early 2000s, AR had become a sophisticated discipline with dedicated professionals, institutional memory, commercial leverage, and in some cases what amounted to competitive intelligence operations directed at analysts themselves.

Executives who understood their own strategic needs — and who trusted the independence and judgment of specific analysts — found ways around the apparatus. Some picked up the phone directly. Some designed social structures that gave analysts plausible cover to participate in informal advisory conversations. Some simply invited analysts into settings where AR could not operate: a private dining room, a home in St. Martin, a couch in Atherton with a cameraman and no prepared remarks.

What Aberdeen's experience ultimately demonstrates is that the most valuable analyst-executive relationships operated at a level that AR could not reach — not because AR was incompetent, but because AR's function was to manage the official relationship, and the official relationship was not where the most useful work happened. The IBM general manager who drove Logan and Kastner to Somers for lunch got something in an afternoon that his organization's official processes could not have delivered in six months. Charles Wang got a CEO introduction that no formal engagement could have produced. Joz got, briefly, the recognition that someone understood what he was building — before the AR system reasserted itself and the moment passed.

The throw-up lunch at Maison Robert was preparation for all of it: for the dinners where the wrong roll placement would signal inexperience, for the wine selections that would communicate judgment or its absence, for the moments when a senior executive leaned over and said something that required a response calibrated not just for accuracy but for the full social and professional weight of the occasion. Getting those moments right was not incidental to the analyst's work. It was the work — the human infrastructure on which the research and the relationships and, ultimately, the value depended.
