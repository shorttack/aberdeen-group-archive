# Chapter 1 — The Stopwatch and the Prod

The best work I did in my Aberdeen years was not a forecast. It was a measurement. For roughly a decade, from the DebitCredit paper of 1985 to the tpmC price/performance tables of 1996, I held a stopwatch to relational database systems and reported what the numbers said, and Aberdeen used those numbers to prod enterprise IS executives off their hardware-first habits and onto a software platform they did not yet trust. That is the thesis of this book, and I want to state it before the evidence rather than after: the relational database became the enterprise's primary software platform because a small number of people insisted on measuring it, and I was one of them. Everything else in the story — the vendor wars, the jungle, the object-relational pivot, the collapse of big iron — follows from the arithmetic.

The archive I am working from supports that claim in a way I did not expect when we started pulling the evidence together. The consolidated Oracle cluster alone carries 406 observations across 74 studies, 345 of them inside the 1970–2005 window [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]. Computer Associates, once you fold in CA-Ingres, is the second-heaviest cluster at 159 observations across 43 studies [study-cab199-1-2-0cb163]. Informix contributes 100 observations across 25 studies; NCR, counting AT&T GIS and Teradata, 85 across 32; Sybase 64 across 25 [study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. The independent Ingres of RTI and ASK is a mere 16 observations across 5 studies, concentrated between 1990 and 1994, and then the evidence simply stops [study-1990-bull-rdbms-1990-and-2024-metadata-fe61bf]. Software AG is 14 observations across 6 studies; DB2 as a product-tagged entity is exactly one observation, and Computer Corporation of America is one observation that falls outside the window entirely [study-aberdeen-1996-software-ag-digital-high-performance-solutions]. I will say more about those two gaps at the end of this chapter, because a longitudinal study that hides its thin spots is a marketing document, not a study.

Read the five-year buckets and the shape of the decade is unmistakable. Relational coverage in our files peaks between 1993 and 1997 — Oracle alone accounts for 64 observations in 1995, 49 in 1996, and 88 in 1997 [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]. Informix runs 3, 13, 11, 19, 39 across 1993 through 1997 and then falls off a cliff to 3 in 1998 [study-informix-universal-server-launch-object-relational-fb2cd4]. Those were the client-server years, the years we called the RDBMS jungle, and they are the center of gravity of this book. Before them lies the benchmark decade. After them lies commoditization. I will take them in order.

## Where the stopwatch came from

Make no mistake: the relational database did not win on elegance. It won on transactions per second, and the discipline of counting transactions per second predates the commercial relational market. The founding document is Tandem Technical Report 85.2, "A Measure of Transaction Processing Power," the DebitCredit paper that Jim Gray and roughly two dozen co-authors published anonymously in 1985 [study-tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a]. Its argument was simple and, at the time, radical: TPS was a standard throughput metric defined to enable cross-vendor comparison, because Whetstones, MIPS, and MegaFLOPS focused on the CPU and did not capture transaction-processing system features [study-tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a]. Anyone who has since read a vendor press release quoting a single clock speed knows exactly what problem Gray was solving.

I was at Stratus then, and the paper landed on my desk as a weapon. I used ET-1 and TP-1 as the standard Stratus-versus-Tandem price/performance comparison framework [study-tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a]. The numbers of that era were small and vivid. Omri Serlin's FTSN-33 report put a 16-CPU Tandem TXP at 100 tps and a ten-system FOX network at the "magic 1000 tps" [study-ftsn-serlin-tandem-txp-et1-benchmarks-19-ea6530]. I compiled the Stratus ET1-versus-TP1 dossier against Tandem in 1986, worked the ET1 functional specification and benchmark set the same year, and edited Stratus Sales Support Competitive News in 1987 [study-stratus-et1-tp1-tandem-dossier-1986-26871a][study-stratus-et1-functional-spec-and-benchmar-0c3172][study-stratus-sales-support-competitive-news-1-2aac8d]. That was competitive selling, not analysis, and I want to be honest about the register: the stopwatch was in my hand because my employer wanted to beat Tandem, not because I had yet developed a theory of the market.

The theory came at Digital. In 1987 I ghostwrote the Yankee Group study "The Future of Transaction Processing," and by 1988 I was inside the DEC benchmark corpus — the Rdb NOTES TP1 thread, the Kohler-Hsu DebitCredit guidelines, Zahavi's VAXcluster DebitCredit work, and my own primer for the Corporate Systems Group on comparing DebitCredit tests [study-dec-notes-tp1-debit-credit-thread-1987-1-5815dc][study-dec-debit-credit-guidelines-kohler-hsu-1-e228b4][study-dec-zahavi-debit-credit-vaxclusters-1988-1a9e2e][study-dec-primer-comparing-debit-credit-tests--c30cfa]. The DECtp launch at the Plaza Hotel in 1988, with Olsen, Kirk, and Glorioso on stage and benchmark charts behind them, was the public face of that work [study-dectp-press-conference-transcript-and-benchmark-charts-plaza-5e5836]. And in August 1988 the Transaction Processing Performance Council was formed, with me at the table as a DEC executive; TPC-A, the standardized DebitCredit-style benchmark, was published in 1989 [study-tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a]. The same year, Aberdeen announced I would head a new Transaction Services business unit [study-aberdeen-press-advisory-kastner-appointm-efd58c]. The stopwatch had become a franchise.

## What I got wrong at Digital

Here is the reckoning, and I would rather put it early than bury it. The richest single OLTP artifact in the archive is the DEC "Blue Monday" internal sales training of 1988, DECtp versus IBM, at 42 observations [study-dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. I told that room that the TP market was $21 billion a year and growing at twice the rate of the overall computer market [study-dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. I told them DEC's debit-credit cost per transaction was less than one-third of an IBM mainframe's and about twice Tandem's, and that a VAX 8830's RAMP-C throughput approached an IBM 3090 Model 200E [study-dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. Those were measurements, and I stand by them. Then I predicted that DEC's TP share would triple from under 5 percent to around 15 percent by the early 1990s [study-dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. It did not. The prescience scorers who later worked through those 42 observations rated the study low, with a mean of 1.95 [ps=2].

I want to be precise about the nature of the error, because it recurs. The arithmetic was right and the forecast was wrong, which means the mistake was not in the stopwatch. It was in assuming that a better price/performance number would, on its own, move share. I was an executive with a product to sell, and I let the product's numbers stand in for the market's behavior. The lesson I took into Aberdeen — and it is the reason this book exists — is that measurement is necessary and not sufficient. Someone has to convert the measurement into a decision the IS executive can actually make. That conversion is what I mean by prodding.

## Open OLTP: the prod finds its audience

Aberdeen's first database franchise was not relational at all. It was open online transaction processing, and it grew directly out of the DEC work. Between 1989 and 1991 I did OLTP market research for BBN Advanced Computers, OLTP market analysis and a follow-on strategy for Encore's Series 90, and a debit-credit benchmark for Bull's DPS 9000 and 7000 lines [study-bbn1989-1e6838][study-encore-b7c40d][study-1991-encore-and-metadata-47f414][study-bull1990-d-c-benchmarks-8b5d9c]. One of my first Aberdeen clients was a mainframe TP1 benchmark audit for Roel Pieper of Software AG [study-aberdeen-1996-software-ag-digital-high-performance-solutions]. I served as a TPC-A and TPC-B benchmark auditor [study-tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a]. The through-line from Stratus to Aberdeen is unbroken: the same stopwatch, now held by an outsider whose only product was the number itself.

The document that turned that practice into a position was "Open Online Transaction Processing: An Enterprise Manager's Guide," which we published in 1991 and which appeared in Korean translation the following year [study-aberdeen-open-online-transaction-process-ea272f][study-aberdeen-open-oltp-enterprise-managers-k-3fc536]. The scorers rated the Korean edition high, with a mean of 2.54 across 13 observations [ps=3]. I lectured the same material as lead speaker at NCR Norge's Open OLTP and RDBMS seminar in Oslo in 1992 [study-ncr-norge-open-oltp-rdbms-seminar-1992-0-7f5414]. Notice what had happened to the argument. At Stratus the question was "whose box is faster." At Aberdeen the question became "what should an enterprise manager buy," and the answer was a benchmarked, open, transaction-processing platform rather than a proprietary one. The relational database walked in through that door.

## 1993: the Report Card and the sea change

If I had to name the single year the market turned, I would name 1993, and I would point to the RDBMS Report Card we published that year [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. The thesis of that report was blunt: IS executives were changing from hardware-oriented to software-platform-oriented decision criteria, and the RDBMS had become the primary software platform for enterprise IS infrastructure, unifying mainframe, midrange, and desktop [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. That is the sea change in one sentence. For twenty years the platform decision had been the hardware decision. From 1993 on, in our view, the platform decision was the database decision, and the hardware followed.

The Report Card also handed out grades, and I want to quote them because some of them did not age well. We said Ingres was best-in-class with all others far behind on part of the scorecard, that Sybase and Ingres were best-in-class on another, and that 1993 marked the first time Oracle demonstrated true technical leadership [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. We reported that a modern RDBMS reduced application development code by up to 35 percent, which is the kind of number an IS executive can take to a budget meeting [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. And we said IBM's DB2 and OS/2 Data Manager were worst-in-class across all platforms, recommending that IBM's new CEO make the RDBMS a top priority [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. The scorers gave the Report Card a high verdict with a mean of 2.00 across 21 observations [ps=2].

Bottom line: we were right about the platform shift and wrong about Ingres. Within eighteen months I would be standing in front of Informix's sales force describing Ingres as the slow, late vehicle that was losing the race, and by 1995 the independent Ingres had disappeared from our evidence, absorbed into Computer Associates [study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a][study-cab199-1-2-0cb163]. Best-in-class technology and a losing company are not a contradiction; they are the most common story in this industry. I should have weighted the company more heavily than the code.

The Report Card did not stand alone. The same 1993 series carried our open-systems status report on the Unix wars, our study of PCs and workstations vying for the 1990s desktop, and our assessment of the mainframe's role, which noted that DB2 did not have the functionality of its Oracle, Ingres, or Sybase midrange competitors [study-nti-2-open-systems-1dd3af][study-nti-3-pc-and-ws-1993-17d49b][study-nti-9-mainframe-role-1993-77fc40]. That year we also told readers the vast majority of desktops would move to MS-DOS 6.0 and Windows 4.0 on the Intel 486, and that the PC was approaching the workstation in client-server and multiuser roles [study-nti-3-pc-and-ws-1993-17d49b]. The hardware was becoming a commodity underneath the database, exactly as the Report Card's framing required.

## 1994–1995: the jungle

The metaphor I am still asked about is the jungle. In my 1994 competitive update for Informix, I described the RDBMS market as a jungle with four vendors on a common playing field, and I gave each of them a vehicle [study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Informix was the Aerostar van — practical, profitable. Sybase was the race car — fast, not yet a daily driver. Ingres was the slow, late vehicle, losing the race [study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Aberdeen then published "Welcome to the RDBMS Jungle" as a consulting report in 1995, chapters one through five and a separate chapter six [study-jungle-1-5-675c58][study-jungle-6-7af8b7]. In it we argued that massively parallel RDBMS systems would eclipse mainframes for large workloads, and we pointed to Walmart running terabyte-class RDBMS workloads as the proof that this was already happening rather than a forecast [study-jungle-1-5-675c58].

Here is a second reckoning, smaller than Blue Monday but worth recording. The Jungle reports carry a high prescience verdict, but their means are the lowest in the relational core — 0.91 across 32 scored observations for chapters one through five and 0.88 across 33 for chapter six [ps=1][ps=1]. The verdict says the direction was right; the means say the individual calls were mostly ordinary. I read that as a fair judgment on a consulting report that was built to sell a framework rather than to make dated predictions, but I will not pretend the vehicle metaphors were analysis. They were teaching. The analysis in those reports was the parallel-scalability argument, and that one held up.

The companion piece was "Can Parallel-Scalable RDBMSs Break the Downsizing Logjam?", also 1995, which the scorers rated high with a mean of 2.67 across 15 observations [study-informdsa-9f8bcf][ps=3]. And the report I would put at the top of the period is "Universal Servers: RDBMS for the Next Decade," our 1995 technology viewpoint that framed the object-relational pivot [study-aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade]. It scored the highest of the relational-core studies, a mean of 2.91 across 22 observations [ps=3]. We noted at the time that DB2's Relational Extenders for text, imaging, audio, and video were not yet deep in the architecture or in sophisticated client-server toolsets [study-aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade]. A year later I was on stage for the Informix Universal Server launch, the first object-relational DBMS, which the archive records as a transcript with 29 observations [study-informix-universal-server-launch-object-relational-fb2cd4]. The ORDBMS pivot is the one place in this decade where I would say Aberdeen was ahead of the market rather than measuring it.

## Why the enterprise had no choice

The prodding worked because the enterprise applications made it work. The canonical case in our files is the 1993 HP 9000 engagement for SAP R/3 at Hercules, 28 observations, which is as clean an example as I know of an application forcing a third-party relational database on a customer [study-1993-hercules-sap-28344a]. Rewriting Hercules' proprietary mainframe database for Unix required Oracle adoption [study-1993-hercules-sap-28344a]. HP was the RISC/Unix reference port for both SAP and Oracle, and Oracle's most important multiuser platform by revenue [study-1993-hercules-sap-28344a]. We warned that applications grow at 30 to 40 percent a year for the first three years after an initial R/3 installation, and we told the client to implement a three-tier-plus topology instead of the planned two-tier, because two-tier had fatal flaws for a distributed enterprise [study-1993-hercules-sap-28344a]. The outcome tag on that study reads that SAP R/3 became the dominant ERP platform worldwide [study-1993-hercules-sap-28344a].

Turn that into the customer's arithmetic. An IS executive in 1993 was not choosing a database because we said so. He was choosing SAP, or Baan, or PeopleSoft, or Siebel, and discovering that the application vendor had already chosen the database class for him. We saw the same mechanism in SAP R/3 on the AS/400, in Sequent's strategic R/3 solution, in Baan's dynamic enterprise modeling, in D&B's SmartStream, and in the Compaq-Siebel alliance [study-aberdeen-1995-ibm-as400-sap-r3][study-sequent-sap-sequen~1-7f70e4][study-aberdeen-1996-baan-changes-rules-erp-game][study-aberdeen-1996-app-dun-bradstreet-software-smartstrtogether-better][study-1997-the-compaq-siebel-global-alliance-an-e-roach-to-cu-458c35]. Where the application and the database vendor pulled in different directions, as SAP and Sybase did in 1996, the tension was news [study-sap-sybase-face-off-f2612e]. The database had become infrastructure, and infrastructure is what you buy when you have already decided to buy something else.

## The stopwatch returns: tpmC

By 1996 the TPC had done what we hoped it would do in 1988: it had made the database-on-platform comparison a matter of published arithmetic. Our study of Digital debunking the NT/SMP scalability myth carried the tpmC rows that I still think of as the decade's clearest snapshot: 6,712 tpmC at $65 per tpmC on a four-way Pentium Pro running Windows NT and Microsoft SQL Server; 8,311 tpmC at $95 per tpmC on four-way UnixWare with Sybase and Tuxedo; 5,774 tpmC at $198 per tpmC on eight-way AIX with Sybase [study-aberdeen-1996-digital-debunks-ntsmp-scalability-myth]. Sequent had scaled NT to 28 processors, and we argued that 64-bit NT would be needed for full high-end capability [study-aberdeen-1996-digital-debunks-ntsmp-scalability-myth][study-1996-sequent-38f0b1]. The scorers rated the NT/SMP study high at a mean of 1.76 across 21 observations [ps=2]. Informix and Silicon Graphics set a TPC-C SMP high-water mark the following year [study-1997-informix-and-silicon-graphics-reach-98e6ea].

Look at those three tpmC rows again. The cheapest transaction was on the commodity platform, and it was cheaper by a factor of three than the proprietary Unix box. That is the whole x86 story in one table, eleven years after Gray's paper said the CPU number was not the point. Our 1996 assessment of the commercial RISC/Unix market found HP dominant, and the scorers gave it a medium verdict with a mean of 1.05 across 55 observations — a low score I read as the market's verdict on the RISC/Unix peak, not on the report [study-aberdeen-1996-risc-unix-market][ps=1]. The highest in-window prescience score in this dossier belongs to a 2003 study in which Dell and Oracle boldly predicted the demise of big iron, at a mean of 3.7 across 10 observations [study-2003-dell-and-oracle-boldly-predict-the-demise-of-big-i-073fba][ps=4]. Compaq's Tru64 Unix with Oracle9i clusters in 2001 and AMD's Opteron at its one-year mark in 2004 bracket the transition [study-2001-compaq-tru64-unix-and-oracle9i-clusters-reduce-cos-17b4b2][study-technology-news-hardware-amd-s-opteron-a-ecf5a9]. The stopwatch that had once compared Stratus to Tandem ended the decade comparing a four-way PC to a mainframe, and the PC won.

## The consultant as outside instrument

I want to close the argument with the engagement that best shows the prod in the customer's hands. In 1995 I helped ITT Hartford make its corporate platform decision, a consulting report of twelve observations that the scorers rated high with a mean of 2.67 [study-itthar-1-4-32f824][ps=3]. The strategic finding was an overwhelming business case for a common IT platform across ITT Hartford's business units [obs-OBS-006]. The platform stack we enumerated ran from the desktop OS through the application and database OS, the network OS, network management, groupware, the 3270 emulator, email, and group calendaring [obs-OBS-008]. The decision, in my judgment, was made systematically and professionally, with tough choices actually made [obs-OBS-007]. The background was the 1994 Commercial Lines Coop review — stay with AT&T cooperation or not — which is the NCR thread that ran through so much of my consulting, including the Oracle and AT&T-NCR "Customer-Driven Information Solutions" tour I keynoted across eight cities in 1993 [obs-OBS-010][study-oracle-att-ncr-customer-driven-seminar-t-b9f539][study-oracle-att-ncr-pittsburgh-seminar-letter-98b0a4].

Two observations from that report are the ones I would have every reader of this book remember. First, the recommendation that a consultant is an objective outsider for high-risk, high-cost decisions where the internal answers conflict [obs-OBS-009]. That is the analyst's entire value proposition, and it is the reason the stopwatch mattered: an outsider's number is worth more than an insider's argument. Second, the paired prediction and outcome. We said Windows 95 would not ship in May 1995 and might be delayed [obs-OBS-011]. It shipped on August 24, 1995, and became the dominant desktop OS [obs-OBS-012]. A dated call, a dated outcome, a score. That is what a longitudinal study is for.

## What this chapter cannot yet say

The honest gaps, so the reader can weigh them. DB2 is under-represented in our files at the product level — one tagged observation — and its real presence rides inside comparison and training studies: the Power Academy and RS/6000 RDBMS sales training I wrote for IBM, the Report Card's worst-in-class grade, the Jungle's later note of DB2's new-found competitiveness in parallel scalability and replication, and a 1997 view that DB2 Universal Database 5.0 would expand share on IBM servers [study-rdbms-for-ibm-powera~1-7a44be][study-ibm-rs6000-midran~1-88f049][study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa][study-jungle-1-5-675c58][study-dbmspr~1-7c854e]. I also served as a bid-protest expert witness on projected poor DB2 performance in the Florida HHS welfare system case, a thread this book returns to later [study-unisys-florida-hrs-expert-witness-tallah-5b4524][study-volume-1-ch09-expert-witness-technology-on-trial-1992-2015]. IBM was the largest overall DBMS supplier throughout, and our DB2 evidence does not yet do that fact justice [study-jungle-1-5-675c58]. Computer Corporation of America — where Wayne Kernochan and my wife Kate both worked — has one observation, dated 2010, outside the window; the Model 204 era is essentially absent from the archive and will have to come from memory [study-computer-corporation-of-america]. DEC Rdb rides the Blue Monday corpus rather than standing alone, and Oracle's later acquisition of Rdb bridges it into the Oracle cluster [study-dec-notes-tp1-debit-credit-thread-1987-1-5815dc]. And the 2026 longitudinal assemblies and memoir chapters I draw on for continuity are synthetic scaffolding, cited here as secondary, never as period sources [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal][study-volume-1-ch08-aberdeen-go-go-years-1998-2006].

So here is where Chapter 1 leaves the argument. The relational database won the enterprise between 1993 and 1997 because it had already won the benchmark between 1985 and 1989, and because the applications the enterprise actually wanted to buy forced the choice. I was wrong about DEC's share and wrong about Ingres, and I was right about the platform shift, the parallel machines, the object-relational turn, and the arithmetic that put a four-way PC ahead of a mainframe. The stopwatch does not care who is holding it. That is why it was the best instrument I ever owned, and why the next eight chapters are about what it measured.
