The relational model did not conquer the enterprise; it was prodded into existence by a chaotic, hardware-obsessed, and deeply skeptical IS community. By the mid-1990s, we had successfully convinced CIOs to abandon their legacy mainframe silos in favor of a "common playing field" defined by Unix, SQL, and client-server topology [jungle-1-5-675c58]. This victory was not inevitable. It required a decade of aggressive market prodding, benchmark auditing, and the painful demolition of vendor myths. In these pages, I recount how we at Aberdeen, alongside our vendors, navigated the "RDBMS jungle" to establish relational databases as the undisputed primary software platform for enterprise infrastructure [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa].

### The Jungle: Defining the Battlefield

The narrative of the 1970–2005 database decade is best understood not as a linear progression of technological superiority, but as a market clearing mechanism. By 1993, IS executives had decisively shifted from hardware-oriented to software-platform-oriented decision criteria [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. The relational database management system (RDBMS) emerged as the primary software platform for enterprise IS infrastructure, unifying mainframe, midrange, and desktop environments [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa].

This unification was messy. As I noted in our "RDBMS Jungle" analysis, the market featured four vendors on a common playing field, each represented by distinct vehicle metaphors that revealed their strategic positions [informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Informix was an Aerostar van: practical and profitable [informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Sybase was a race car: fast, but not yet a reliable daily driver [informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Ingres was a slow or late vehicle, losing the race [informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. And Oracle? Oracle was the one sustaining its presence across the entire arc, evolving from a niche Unix player into the dominant force [jungle-1-5-675c58].

The adoption arc reveals a specific window of intensity. RDBMS coverage peaked between 1993 and 1997, the era I labeled the "client-server/RDBMS jungle years" [jungle-1-5-675c58]. During this period, Oracle sustained its growth, while Informix saw a dramatic rise in observations from 1994 to 1997 before declining [dossier-table-chapter-1-2-rdbms-adoption-arc]. Sybase experienced a peak in 1995 and 1996 but collapsed thereafter [dossier-table-chapter-1-2-rdbms-adoption-arc]. Ingres evidence concentrated heavily between 1990 and 1994, collapsing immediately after its acquisition by CA [dossier-table-chapter-1-2-rdbms-adoption-arc].

This peak was not accidental. It was driven by the convergence of hardware shifts and the emergence of object-relational architectures. In 1995, I framed the RDBMS as the "Universal Server" for the next decade [aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade]. This framing highlighted the pivot toward object-relational (ORDBMS) capabilities, a move that promised to bridge the gap between rigid relational schemas and complex application needs [informix-universal-server-launch-object-relational-fb2cd4]. The prescience score for this "Universal Server" thesis was high (mean 2.91), reflecting our accurate prediction that ORDBMS features would become critical for enterprise scalability [aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade].

### The Prodding: Benchmarks, OLTP, and the TPC

The relational model’s ascent was fueled by a specific mechanism: Open Online Transaction Processing (OLTP). Before RDBMS could dominate high-end workloads, it had to prove it could handle transactional throughput. This was my domain. At DEC, in 1988, I ghostwrote internal sales training materials that aggressively compared our VAX 8830 RDP-C performance against IBM mainframes and Tandem systems [dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. We claimed DEC debit-credit cost-per-transaction was less than one-third that of IBM mainframes and roughly double Tandem’s [dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. We predicted DEC’s TP share would triple from under 5% to 15% by the early 1990s [dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc].

That prediction was optimistic. The prescience score for the "Blue Monday" training is low (mean 1.95), a testament to the gap between authored optimism and market reality [dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. The transaction processing market, which I estimated at $21 billion per year in 1988, grew differently than anticipated [dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. However, the act of prodding was successful. By defining benchmarks like ET-1 and TP-1, and later participating in the founding of the Transaction Processing Performance Council (TPC) in August 1988, we established a standard for cross-vendor comparison [tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a].

The TPC-A standardized DebitCredit-style benchmarks were published in 1989, followed by TPC-C, which became the high-water mark for SMP performance [tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a]. This benchmarking infrastructure forced vendors to improve. Ingres demonstrated best-in-class technical leadership early on [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa], while Oracle demonstrated true technical leadership by 1993 [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. By the late 1990s, Informix and Silicon Graphics reached new high-water marks for TPC-C tpmC price/performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of our prodding [1997-informix-and-silicon-graphics-reach-98e6ea].

My role as a consultant and auditor was central to this process. At BBN in 1989, I conducted OLTP market research [bbn1989-1e6838]. At Encore, I analyzed the Series 90 market [encore-b7c40d]. For Bull, I benchmarked the DPS 9000/7000 [bull1990-d-c-benchmarks-8b5d9c]. And in 1991, I published "Open Online Transaction Processing: An Enterprise Manager's Guide," a paper that gained traction enough to be translated into Korean [aberdeen-open-online-transaction-process-ea272f] [aberdeen-open-oltp-enterprise-managers-k-3fc536]. This work established Aberdeen’s "Transaction Services" business unit and solidified my reputation as an objective outsider who could help enterprises make high-risk platform decisions [aberdeen-press-advisory-kastner-appointm-efd58c] [itthar-1-4-32f824].

### The Platform Decision: ITT Hartford and the Common IT Platform

The prodding of vendors was only half the battle; we had to persuade enterprises to adopt these new stacks. The ITT Hartford case study (1995) stands as a canonical exemplar of this process [itthar-1-4-32f824]. In 1994, ITT Hartford faced a critical question: Stay with AT&T Cooperation or move toward NCR? The decision framework required an objective outsider [itthar-1-4-32f824].

Our recommendation was pragmatic: a dual workgroup-OS strategy of Windows NT/AS and Novell NetWare [itthar-1-4-32f824]. We argued that Windows 95, despite delays in shipping (OBS-011), would become the runaway desktop OS due to its huge ISV support and 16-bit compatibility [itthar-1-4-32f824]. We warned that IBM OS/2 Warp had little ISV support or commitment [itthar-1-4-32f824]. The outcome was accurate: Windows 95 shipped on August 24, 1995, and became the dominant desktop OS [itthar-1-4-32f824].

More importantly, ITT Hartford made an "overwhelming business case" for a Common IT Platform across its business units [itthar-1-4-32f824]. This platform stack included desktop OS, app/DB OS, network OS, and groupware [itthar-1-4-32f824]. The adoption of a common platform necessitated the adoption of third-party RDBMS services, as proprietary mainframe databases could not scale across this distributed topology. This realization was confirmed by our 1995 "Universal Servers" study, which scored highly for its prescience [aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade].

### The Enterprise Stack: SAP, Baan, and the Death of Big Iron

The relational database became indispensable as enterprise applications migrated to client-server architectures. The "Hercules" case (1993) is the definitive proof point: rewriting a proprietary mainframe database for Unix required the adoption of Oracle [1993-hercules-sap-28344a]. HP’s 9000 became the RISC/Unix reference port for both SAP and Oracle, marking Oracle’s most important multiuser platform in terms of revenue [1993-hercules-sap-28344a].

As applications like SAP R/3 were installed, they grew at 30-40% per year for the first three years [1993-hercules-sap-28344a]. This growth exposed the "fatal flaws" of two-tier client-server architectures, forcing enterprises to adopt a three-tier-plus topology [1993-hercules-sap-28344a]. This shift benefited Sybase and Oracle, who offered robust RDBMS solutions for ERP workloads [sap-sybase-face-off-f2612e] [aberdeen-1995-ibm-as400-sap-r3]. Baan ERP also drove demand, with "Dynamic Enterprise Modeling" gaining traction in 1996 [aberdeen-1996-baan-changes-rules-erp-game].

The reliance on third-party RDBMS extended to financial and CRM applications. Dun & Bradstreet’s SmartStream Distributed Enterprise (1996) required a scalable database backend [aberdeen-1996-app-dun-bradstreet-software-smartstrtogether-better] [aberdeen-1996-app-dun-bradstreet-software-smartstrtogether-better]. Similarly, the Compaq-Siebel Global Alliance (1997) tied CRM application success to underlying database performance [1997-the-compaq-siebel-global-alliance-an-e-roach-to-cu-458c35] [aberdeen-1996-app-dun-bradstreet-software-smartstrtogether-better].

This ecosystem created a self-reinforcing loop: enterprise apps demanded scalable RDBMS, which drove hardware innovation (Unix SMP, x86 clusters), which in turn enabled more complex applications. By 2003, this loop had reached its zenith. Dell and Oracle boldly predicted the demise of big iron [2003-dell-and-oracle-boldly-predict-the-demise-of-big-i-073fba]. This prediction scored high on prescience (mean 3.7/10), reflecting the successful commoditization of x86 hardware and the migration of large workloads to Unix/Linux clusters [2003-dell-and-oracle-boldly-predict-the-demise-of-big-i-073fba]. The "jungle" had been tamed, replaced by a clear hierarchy where Oracle, Sybase, and Informix dominated the midrange and high-end, while IBM’s DB2 struggled to keep up in transactional throughput.

### Honest Reckoning: Where I Was Wrong

The narrative of the database decade is often read as a linear victory for relational databases. However, an honest reckoning requires admitting where my prescience failed. The "Blue Monday" sales training (1988) was overly optimistic about DEC’s ability to capture transaction processing market share from IBM and Tandem [dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc]. The TPC benchmarks I helped structure highlighted technical excellence, but they did not account for the massive inertia of proprietary mainframe ecosystems. DEC failed to triple its share; instead, Oracle and Sybase absorbed much of the midrange market.

Furthermore, my early framing of Ingres as a "slow/late vehicle" [informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a] underestimated CA’s ability to integrate it into their Unicenter/Jasmine suite. By 1997, CA had significant influence (42 observations), and the Ingres product line was effectively absorbed into the CA ecosystem [cab199-1-2-0cb163] [ENT-CA-INGRES]. My failure to predict this consolidation left me focused on the "big four" (Oracle, Informix, Sybase, DB2) while underestimating the niche but lucrative influence of CA and Software AG.

Finally, the prediction that "massively parallel RDBMS systems will eclipse mainframes for large workloads" [informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a] was directionally correct but temporally aggressive. While SMP clusters grew rapidly (e.g., Sequent scaling to 28 CPUs [1996-sequent-38f0b1]), the "demise of big iron" was not fully realized until the early 2000s, validated by the Dell/Oracle prediction in 2003 [2003-dell-and-oracle-boldly-predict-the-demise-of-big-i-073fba]. The transition was slower than the "jungle" years suggested.

### The Remainder: CA, Software AG, and IBM DB2

The "80/20 rule" applies to the database market as well. Approximately 80% of the RDBMS core was comprised of Oracle, Informix, Sybase, and DB2 [dossier-table-chapter-1-2-rdbms-adoption-arc]. The remaining 20% included Software AG, CA, IBM (as a DB vendor), and CCA.

Software AG (14 observations) was a minor but persistent player, often mentioned alongside Informix and Oracle in lead lists [aberdeen-1996-software-ag-digital-high-performance-solutions] [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. CA (Computer Associates), however, was a major force. With 159 observations, CA’s presence peaked in 1997 and 2001 [dossier-table-chapter-1-2-rdbms-adoption-arc] [cab199-1-2-0cb163]. Our deep relationship with CA included sales training (1997, 25 obs) and retention contracts [cab199-1-2-0cb163] [1999-retainer-contract-d3a385]. CA-Ingres bridged the gap between the standalone Ingres story and the broader CA ecosystem [ENT-CA-INGRES].

IBM DB2 presents a complex case. Despite being tagged in only one product-level study (ent-ibm-db2), DB2 was embedded in many comparison and training studies [ent-ibm-db2] [rdbms-for-ibm-powera~1-7a44be]. In the 1993 "RDBMS Report Card," DB2 was rated "worst-in-class across all platforms" [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. By 1996, however, DB2 for AIX V2 was being bundled with hardware and showing improvements in replication and scalability [rdbms-for-ibm-powera~1-7a44be] [jungle-6-7af8b7]. Despite these efforts, DB2 remained a follower in functionality and performance compared to Oracle, Sybase, and Informix [aberdeen-1996-ibm-transformation-business-machines-information-service]. The "gap" in DB2’s representation in our studies is significant; much of its presence was qualitative, embedded in training transcripts rather than quantitative benchmark outputs [ent-ibm-db2] [rdbms-for-ibm-powera~1-7a44be].

CCA (Computer Corporation of America) was sparse, with only one observation in 2010, outside our primary window. The Model 204 story remains largely unrecorded in the archive, requiring manual sourcing or memory retrieval [computer-corporation-of-america]. This gap reflects the archival bias toward "commercial RDBMS on Unix" and away from earlier, niche mainframe databases.

### Conclusion: The Database Decade

The 1970–2005 database decade was a period of intense market prodding. Through benchmarks like TPC-A and -C, consulting engagements like ITT Hartford, and aggressive vendor analysis, we helped enterprises adopt the relational model as their primary software platform [nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa] [itthar-1-4-32f824]. The "jungle" of the mid-1990s gave way to a clearer hierarchy, with Oracle leading the pack, Sybase and Informix fighting for the middle, and IBM DB2 struggling to catch up.

This victory was not guaranteed. It required the demotion of proprietary mainframe databases, the rise of Unix SMP hardware, and the emergence of three-tier enterprise architectures. As we moved into the 2000s, the "demise of big iron" became a reality, validated by Dell and Oracle’s predictions [2003-dell-and-oracle-boldly-predict-the-demise-of-big-i-073fba]. The relational database had won, not by default, but through a decade of relentless prodding, auditing, and architectural refinement.

The lessons learned in this period—about the importance of open standards, the power of benchmarks, and the necessity of objective consultation—remain relevant today. As we look back on the "Database Decade," we see not just a technological shift, but a market transformation driven by a community of vendors, consultants, and enterprises who chose to prodded the status quo into a new reality. The jungle was tamed, the platform was defined, and the database decade began.
