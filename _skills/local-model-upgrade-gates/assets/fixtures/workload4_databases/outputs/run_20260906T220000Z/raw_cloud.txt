The RDBMS jungle was the phase of my career when I stopped being an analyst who wrote about databases and became an analyst the database vendors argued in front of. Between 1993 and 1997, Aberdeen published — and I authored or co-authored — the studies that Oracle, Informix, Sybase, Ingres, and the second-rank vendors used to explain themselves to their customers, their boards, and each other. The evidence dossier for this chapter reflects that concentration. Oracle draws 345 in-window observations across 74 studies [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]; Informix draws 98 across 25 [study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]; CA (as the eventual owner of the Ingres codebase) draws 113 across 43 [study-cab199-1-2-0cb163]; Sybase draws 53 across 25; independent Ingres draws only 15 across five — the collapse curve of a company acquired by CA in 1994 rendered numerically visible.

The core argument I made in that window, and the one I would make again with the benefit of the dossier's prescience scoring, was that the RDBMS became the primary software platform for enterprise IS infrastructure and that IS executives had begun to choose the database first and let the database dictate the hardware. That's the thesis of the 1993 RDBMS Report Card [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa], scored high in the prescience layer with mean 2.00 across 21 observations [ps=2]. The Report Card called it plainly: "IS executives changing from hardware-oriented to software-platform-oriented decision criteria" and "RDBMS = primary software platform for enterprise IS infrastructure; unifies mainframe, midrange, and desktop." Every subsequent client-server engagement in the archive rides on that inversion.

The Report Card was also where I planted my flag on per-vendor merit — "Ingres best-in-class; all others far behind," "First time Oracle demonstrated true technical leadership," "Sybase and Ingres best-in-class" — and where I first quoted the productivity number that appears in every subsequent RDBMS pitch: "Reduces application development code by up to 35%." What I got wrong in the Report Card was Oracle's inevitability. I gave Ingres the technical crown at exactly the moment CA was preparing to buy it and freeze it. My best-in-class technical vendor was already dead. Salesforce and platform economics decided the market, not technical merit. I would spend the next four years watching Larry Ellison prove that in the field.

The vehicle metaphors that made the "RDBMS Jungle" studies famous [study-jungle-1-5-675c58][study-jungle-6-7af8b7] were an attempt to make that reality legible to CIOs who wanted a technical decision and were being handed a commercial one. "Informix = Aerostar van (practical, profitable). Sybase = race car (fast, not yet a daily driver). Ingres = slow/late vehicle — losing the race." Those two Jungle studies together carry 41 observations and score high in the prescience layer (mean 0.91 and 0.88 respectively) [ps=1] — not because the metaphors themselves were prescient but because the underlying reading of the market was. Informix's practicality carried it into 1996-97 as the object-relational bet that Universal Server was supposed to cash [study-informix-universal-server-launch-object-relational-fb2cd4]. Sybase's speed carried it into the Wall Street trading rooms and then out of them. And Ingres, as I said, was already gone.

The one Jungle prediction that landed clean was on parallel scale. "Massively parallel RDBMS systems will eclipse mainframes for large workloads." "Walmart running terabyte-class RDBMS workloads." I was writing that in 1994-95 with SP2 and Sequent and Sybase MPP as the reference cases. By 1997 the TPC-C high-water marks had moved to Informix on SGI Origin [study-1997-informix-and-silicon-graphics-reach-98e6ea], and by 2003 Dell and Oracle were on the cover of the trades saying the mainframe was over [study-2003-dell-and-oracle-boldly-predict-the-demise-of-big-i-073fba] — the highest prescience score in the whole dossier at 3.7 [ps=4]. The line from "massively parallel RDBMS eclipses mainframes" to "Dell and Oracle predict the demise of big iron" is a straight one, and it's mine.

The second thing I want to argue is that the RDBMS story cannot be told without the OLTP benchmarking story, and the benchmarking story is mine in a way the vendor story is not. The 1985 Tandem Technical Report 85.2 [study-tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a] is the DebitCredit paper — Jim Gray plus twenty-four other authors defining transactions per second as the cross-vendor comparison metric that CPU-oriented Whetstones and MegaFLOPS could not capture. I was at Stratus at the time, and we used ET-1 and TP-1 as the standard framework for arguing Stratus-versus-Tandem price/performance. The dossier records this straightforwardly and I don't need to embellish it: "Kastner (then at Stratus) used ET-1/TP-1 as the standard Stratus-vs-Tandem price/performance comparison framework." The Serlin FTSN-33 paper [obs-ftsn-serlin-tandem-txp-et1-benchmarks-19-ea6530] gave us the "16-CPU TXP can do 100 tps; 10-system FOX network can do 'magic 1000 tps'" number that anchored the whole 1985-86 conversation.

What we built at Stratus in 1986 [study-stratus-et1-tp1-tandem-dossier-1986-26871a][study-stratus-et1-functional-spec-and-benchmar-0c3172][study-stratus-sales-support-competitive-news-1-2aac8d] was the compiler-and-editor's view of that competitive space, and I was the compiler. In 1987 I ghostwrote the Yankee Group's "The Future of Transaction Processing." In 1988 I went to DEC, wrote the "Blue Monday" internal sales training [study-dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc], and made the calls I now cite honestly as wrong. Blue Monday carries 42 observations — the richest single OLTP artifact in the dossier — and scores low, mean 1.95 [ps=2]. The calls: "TP market $21 billion/year, growing 2× rate of overall computer market" (correct). "DEC debit-credit cost-per-transaction <1/3 IBM mainframe, ~2× Tandem" (correct at the time, quickly obsolete). "VAX 8830 RAMP-C TP throughput approaches IBM 3090 Model 200E" (correct and short-lived). "Kastner predicts DEC TP share will triple from <5% to ~15% by early 1990s" (wrong — DEC never made 15%; Compaq bought DEC in 1998 and HP inherited the corpse). Blue Monday is a Path-B artifact: authored optimism versus what actually happened.

The DECtp press conference at the Plaza in 1988 [study-dectp-press-conference-transcript-and-benchmark-charts-plaza-5e5836] and the DEC benchmark corpus I authored and co-authored that year [study-dec-primer-comparing-debit-credit-tests--c30cfa] were the point at which the DEC transaction-processing franchise was launched and the point at which the arithmetic of "DEC will take 15%" started to look strained. Two months later I left DEC to help found Aberdeen, and one month after that, in August 1988, the TPC was formed with me as the DEC delegate. The TPC founding is where the benchmarking-vendor-cycle becomes the industry-standard-audit-cycle, and where my career pivots from being a beneficiary of unaudited numbers to being a producer of audited ones.

That producer's role — TPC-A and TPC-B auditor, "Open OLTP: An Enterprise Manager's Guide" author [study-aberdeen-open-online-transaction-process-ea272f], the Korean-translated version [study-aberdeen-open-oltp-enterprise-managers-k-3fc536] scored high at mean 2.54 across 13 observations [ps=3] — is what I want any reader of this chapter to understand about my presence in the RDBMS jungle. I was not there as a neutral analyst. I was there as a benchmark auditor who had reasons to believe the vendors' numbers were the story. The Open OLTP guide's prescience score of 2.54 reflects the fact that "open OLTP" — the idea that transaction processing did not have to live inside proprietary mainframe transaction monitors and could ride on Unix and later on x86 — is one of the calls that landed.

The third argument I want to make in this chapter, and the one where I need to be most careful with the prescience layer, is that the Universal Servers pivot — the ORDBMS bet [study-aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade] scored high at mean 2.91 across 22 observations [ps=3] and the parallel-scalable RDBMS piece [study-informdsa-9f8bcf] scored high at mean 2.67 across 15 observations [ps=3] — was the argument I made in 1995 that databases would absorb everything: text, images, spatial data, video, whatever the next application domain needed. The core evidence was Informix's Universal Server object-relational launch [obs-informix-universal-server-launch-object-relational-fb2cd4], Oracle's Network Computing Architecture [study-aberdeen-1996-oracle-network-computing-architecture], and the ORDBMS framing across the 1995-96 Aberdeen studies.

I was half right on universal servers. The absorption thesis was correct — databases did become the substrate for text search (Oracle Text, DB2 Text Extender), for spatial data (PostGIS eventually, Oracle Spatial commercially), for JSON documents (the whole 2010s NoSQL detour returning to relational storage engines), and for the multi-modal indexing that AI retrieval pipelines now depend on. I was wrong on the vendor. Informix did not become the ORDBMS leader because IBM bought Informix in 2001 and reoriented it around IBM's DB2 strategy. Oracle and PostgreSQL are the ORDBMS leaders now. Sybase, briefly on the object-relational bandwagon in the mid-90s [obs-sybase-xi-launch-boats-analogy-multiple-databases-79c9ee], became SAP's captive database. What survived from Universal Servers was the architectural idea, not the market position I predicted for it.

The fourth argument is the one I only have space to sketch here because the dossier is explicit that the third-party-RDBMS-behind-the-app story is a chapter of its own. Every major enterprise application in the 1993-2003 window forced the customer to buy someone else's database. SAP forced Oracle in the Hercules SAP R/3 case study [study-1993-hercules-sap-28344a] with the plain finding "Rewriting proprietary mainframe database to Unix required Oracle adoption" and the platform corollary "HP is RISC/Unix reference port for both SAP and Oracle; Oracle's most important multiuser platform in revenue." Baan forced RDBMS choice through Dynamic Enterprise Modeling [study-aberdeen-1996-baan-changes-rules-erp-game]. D&B forced it through SmartStream [study-aberdeen-1996-app-dun-bradstreet-software-smartstrtogether-better]. Siebel via the Compaq alliance forced platform-plus-database as a bundled decision [study-1997-the-compaq-siebel-global-alliance-an-e-roach-to-cu-458c35]. The mechanism was identical across ERP, CRM, and financials: the application shipped without a database and the customer's platform team had to pick one. My analyst work in that period was largely about telling those platform teams what to pick.

I want to close on what the dossier's data-quality notes signal about what I still have to do to finish this chapter honestly. DB2 is under-represented at the product-tag level (one observation on `ent-ibm-db2`), but the DB2 story rides inside every RDBMS-comparison study I wrote in 1993-97 — Power Academy RDBMS training [study-rdbms-for-ibm-powera~1-7a44be], RS/6000 midrange [study-ibm-rs6000-midran~1-88f049], the Report Card's "Worst-in-class across all platforms (DB2, OS/2 Data Manager)" finding [obs-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. The DB2 story is that IBM had the deepest RDBMS engineering bench in the industry and could not ship a competitive commercial DB2 for AIX until 1995-96, by which point Oracle owned the Unix RDBMS installed base. The 1992 Florida HRS welfare-system bid protest I served as expert witness on — where I testified that projected DB2 performance for the workload was inadequate — is a Path-B artifact of the same failure [obs-volume-1-ch09-expert-witness-technology-on-trial-1992-2015].

CCA — Computer Corporation of America, the Model 204 vendor, where Wayne Kernochan worked and where my wife Kate worked — is essentially absent from the archive (one observation, 2010, out of window). That's not a defect of the analysis; it's a defect of the source corpus. The 1970s-80s CCA story belongs in the memoir, not in the archival synthesis. I mention it here so that any reader who expects to find CCA in this chapter understands why it is not here.

The through-line, if there is one, is this: the RDBMS decade was the decade in which enterprise IT stopped being a hardware decision. The 1993 Report Card said it. The Jungle studies dramatized it with vehicle metaphors. Universal Servers argued the corollary that databases would absorb every new data domain. The OLTP-benchmarking arc from DebitCredit through the TPC to the 2003 "demise of big iron" call gave the whole story an audited spine. I got individual vendors wrong repeatedly — Ingres, Informix, DEC's TP share — because I mistook technical merit and honest benchmarking for the drivers of market outcome. What I got right, and what the prescience layer of the dossier confirms, was the shape of the decade: the database becoming the platform, the platform becoming portable off proprietary hardware, and the audited benchmark becoming the way IT bought either one.
