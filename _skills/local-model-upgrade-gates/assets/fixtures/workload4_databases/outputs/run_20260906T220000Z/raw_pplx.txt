# The RDBMS Adoption Arc

The relational database went from an academic conjecture to the default enterprise data platform in roughly a decade, and Aberdeen made a business out of prodding that transition along. When I look back at what we actually shipped between 1990 and 1997 — the report cards, the Jungle taxonomies, the vendor-specific competitive briefings, the OLTP evangelism — the through-line is not that we predicted the winner. Oracle's dominance was not a call any of us needed to make [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]. The through-line is that we told IS executives, again and again, that the software platform now mattered more than the hardware, and that a well-chosen RDBMS would unify their mainframe, midrange, and desktop tiers into one addressable estate [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa][ps=2]. That framing shift — hardware-centric to software-platform-centric decision criteria — is the thing I most want future readers to understand about the decade.

The evidence density in the archive tells the same story. Across the six clusters that make up the relational core — Oracle, Informix, Sybase, Ingres, CA-Ingres, and the fragmented DB2 evidence — the observation counts peak sharply in 1993–1997, the client-server "Jungle" years, then thin out as consolidation set in [study-cab199-1-2-0cb163]. Oracle alone accumulates 345 in-window observations across 74 studies [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]. Informix contributes 98 observations before the 1997 collapse pulls the curve down [study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Sybase runs 53 observations concentrated in the 1993–1997 spike and then falls off [study-1997-migrating-from-sybase-sql-server-4.-d7b1d0]. Independent Ingres barely clears 15 observations before Computer Associates absorbs it [study-ingres-windows-4gl-1990-gui-development-tools-75ade0]. These are not neutral totals. They are the shape of our attention, and our attention was tracking where enterprise buyers were actually spending their software budget.

## The Report Card thesis

The 1993 RDBMS Report Card is the piece I would still hand to a new analyst who wanted to understand what Aberdeen was for [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa][ps=2]. The document does two things at once. It scores the vendors per criterion — "Ingres best-in-class; all others far behind" on architectural discipline, "First time Oracle demonstrated true technical leadership" on the current release, "Sybase and Ingres best-in-class" on client-server tooling, "Worst-in-class across all platforms" for DB2 and OS/2 Data Manager [obs-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa-scorecard][study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. And it makes an editorial move that mattered more than any per-vendor call: it declared that "RDBMS = primary software platform for enterprise IS infrastructure," and that a properly deployed relational system "reduces application development code by up to 35%" [obs-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa-thesis][study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. The scorecard was the retail packaging; the platform thesis was the actual product.

I want to be honest about what the Report Card got wrong. We had Ingres at the top on architecture in 1993, and by 1994 the company was effectively out of the independent race and heading for the CA acquisition that closed later that year [study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa][study-cab199-1-2-0cb163]. Architectural quality did not translate into commercial survival at any of the moments we most expected it to, and Ingres was the first and clearest case in the archive. The lesson I took forward — and that shows up in the Jungle work the following year — was that per-criterion excellence is not the same as the ability to execute a full commercial program across sales, services, and platform partnerships.

## Welcome to the RDBMS Jungle

The 1994–1995 "Welcome to the RDBMS Jungle" chapters and the accompanying Kastner competitive-update addresses were the frame I most wanted to be remembered by [study-jungle-1-5-675c58][study-jungle-6-7af8b7][ps=1]. The premise was straightforward: "the RDBMS market is a 'jungle' with four vendors on a common playing field," and picking a winner from technical merit alone was going to keep leading people wrong [obs-jungle-1-5-675c58-jungle-thesis][study-jungle-1-5-675c58]. So I gave the vendors vehicle metaphors that a room full of tired IS directors could remember at 3 pm. Informix was "an Aerostar van — practical, profitable," the workhorse that would move the load and get paid for it [obs-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a-aerostar][study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Sybase was "a race car — fast, not yet a daily driver," which pinned the technical achievement and the operational risk in the same breath [obs-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a-racecar][study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. Ingres was "a slow or late vehicle — losing the race," which turned out to be the polite framing of what was actually a rout [obs-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a-ingres][study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a].

Two forecasts inside the Jungle work matter more than the metaphors. The first: "Massively parallel RDBMS systems will eclipse mainframes for large workloads" [obs-jungle-6-7af8b7-parallel][study-jungle-6-7af8b7][ps=1]. The second, which I remember getting into an argument about after a keynote: "Walmart running terabyte-class RDBMS workloads" as a live proof point rather than a slideware promise [obs-jungle-1-5-675c58-walmart][study-jungle-1-5-675c58]. Both propositions were controversial when we published them and both were routine by 2001. The Jungle work is my favorite piece of Aberdeen output from the decade because it did the two things I now think an analyst's report should always try to do: give the buyer a decision heuristic they can remember without reading the report again, and stake a testable claim about where the market is going.

## Oracle: the sustained arc

Oracle is the only vendor whose evidence trail runs the full width of the window without a collapse [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]. Our first tagged Oracle observation lands in 1977 — the founding year — and the coverage climbs from four observations in 1988 to nineteen in 1993, then jumps to sixty-four in 1995 and eighty-eight in 1997 before the archive shifts its center of gravity toward platforms and applications [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]. What we were writing about Oracle changed shape with the company. The 1995 Oracle InterOffice study is a product-messaging piece anchored in thirty-nine observations about applications riding a relational core [study-aberdeen-1995-oracle-interoffice]. The 1996 Oracle Network Computing Architecture study, at twenty-nine observations, is where I first tried to argue that Oracle's platform ambitions had moved past the database and were competing with Microsoft's application-server framing [study-aberdeen-1996-oracle-network-computing-architecture]. The 1997 Oracle8 study, at twenty observations, and the Oracle Developer/2000 study, at sixteen, put a bookmark on the "database for network computing" pitch that carried Oracle through the internet buildout [study-1997-oracle8-the-database-for-network-computing-674c9d][study-1997-oracle-developer-2000-client-server-development-en-5f98ce].

The single most consequential Oracle-adjacent call in the archive, though, sits inside the 1995 "Universal Servers: RDBMS for the Next Decade" study [study-aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade][ps=3]. That was where we said the object-relational pivot — extending the relational engine to handle text, spatial, multimedia, and unstructured payloads — was going to be the shape of the "RDBMS for the next decade" [obs-aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade-thesis][study-aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade]. The prescience layer scores that study at 2.91 mean across 22 evaluated observations, high verdict [ps=3]. What I did not predict correctly was how much of the object-relational promise would arrive as bolted-on Extenders rather than as a rewritten core; the ORDBMS message was directionally right and architecturally optimistic, and I would give the same call again with less confidence.

## Informix, the object-relational pivot, and the fall

Informix is the vendor whose story I still find the hardest to tell straight, because the technical work was genuinely first and the commercial outcome was genuinely last. The 1996 Informix Universal Server launch, which I covered live and wrote up at twenty-nine observations, was the first shipping object-relational DBMS on a Unix platform of consequence [study-informix-universal-server-launch-object-relational-fb2cd4][ps=2]. The 1997 Informix–SGI TPC-C benchmark work put the joint stack at SMP high-water marks that Oracle and Sybase had not yet posted [study-1997-informix-and-silicon-graphics-reach-98e6ea]. The 1997 MetaCube 4.0 launch extended the relational engine into OLAP territory that would later be ceded to purpose-built cubes [study-1997-informix-metacube-4.0--relational-o-23491d]. The 1995 "Can Parallel-Scalable RDBMSs Break the Downsizing Logjam?" study, which we scored high on prescience at 2.67 across 15 evaluated observations, argued that the parallel Informix DSA architecture was the mechanism by which mainframe workloads would in fact come down to Unix clusters [study-informdsa-9f8bcf][ps=3].

None of that stopped the 1997 accounting restatement, the customer flight, or the 2001 IBM acquisition [study-1997-migrating-from-sybase-sql-server-4.-d7b1d0]. If the Report Card was where I first argued that architecture drives outcomes, Informix is the counter-example I have to live with. Being first with the object-relational engine, first with the tightly-integrated OLAP cube, and first with a credible SMP benchmark stack was not enough to survive a governance failure. The archive keeps the Informix curve honest: 25 studies, 98 in-window observations, and a coverage cliff that begins in 1997 and never recovers [study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a].

## Sybase: the race car that stayed in the pit lane

Sybase is the cluster where the vehicle metaphor did the most work [obs-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a-racecar][study-informix-competitive-update-kastner-rdbms-jungle-25604a]. The 64-observation Sybase footprint concentrates in 1993–1997 and then trails off, exactly the profile of a vendor whose peak technical reputation ran ahead of its operational credibility [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal]. The 1996 "SAP, Sybase Face Off" news coverage captured the moment SAP began to explicitly signal to the ERP buyer base that Sybase was not going to be a first-tier database partner for R/3 workloads, and that Oracle would be [study-sap-sybase-face-off-f2612e]. That was the call, in retrospect, that ended Sybase as a general-purpose enterprise RDBMS story and left it to survive as a specialized OLTP and financial-services engine.

## Ingres and CA-Ingres

I want to give Ingres the paragraph its architectural work deserves without softening the commercial outcome. The 1990 Bull-sponsored Ingres/Sybase customer-satisfaction survey, one of my early Aberdeen pieces, put Ingres ahead on architectural discipline and customer satisfaction and Sybase ahead on new-workload performance [study-1990-bull-rdbms-1990-and-2024-metadata-fe61bf]. The 1990 Ingres Windows 4GL launch, which I attended in person, is preserved as a transcript because the GUI-development framing genuinely was ahead of what the other vendors were talking about at the time [study-ingres-windows-4gl-1990-gui-development-tools-75ade0]. By 1994 the ASK Group parent was in trouble, the CA acquisition was closing, and the Ingres coverage in our archive drops to zero for the next several years [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal].

CA-Ingres, on the other hand, is a case where I had a direct commercial relationship with the vendor, and I want to be transparent about that. My 1997 CA RDBMS Market Overview, delivered as sales training and preserved at 25 observations, is a Kastner-authored piece for a vendor that was paying us [study-cab199-1-2-0cb163][ps=2]. The 1996 Unicenter/Jasmine piece and the 1997 Unicenter TNG framework piece are also part of the CA retainer relationship [study-carepo-kastner-unicenter-jasmine-1996-1d5ffe][study-1997-ca-s-unicenter-tng-framework-pk-apr-50d15f]. The 1999 retainer contract itself is in the archive, and the letter to Sanjay Kumar at CA World 2001 marks the tail of that relationship [study-1999-retainer-contract-d3a385][study-letter-to-sanjay-kumar-ca-world-2001-d60b1b]. The evidence is what it is, and readers should weigh the CA-branded work against the independent Report Card and Jungle work with that context in view.

## DB2, and what the archive under-represents

DB2 is the vendor sub-arc where I have to be most careful, because the archive undercounts it. At the product-tagged level, `ent-ibm-db2` carries only one observation across the whole window [study-ent-ibm-db2]. The real DB2 evidence lives inside our RDBMS comparison studies and the IBM training work we did — the 1996 Power Academy RDBMS sales training, which I authored at 22 observations, and the RS/6000 midrange training study [study-rdbms-for-ibm-powera~1-7a44be][study-ibm-rs6000-midran~1-88f049]. The Report Card's "worst-in-class across all platforms" verdict on DB2 in 1993 is one of the harshest calls in the archive [obs-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa-db2-worst][study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa]. The Jungle Ch. 6 update from 1995 walks a lot of that back — DB2 for AIX 2, Data Propagator, HACMP, SP2 scalability, and stored procedures had closed enough of the gap to make DB2 a credible large-workload contender by then [study-jungle-6-7af8b7]. And by the 1995 IBM DB2 Common Server profile the vendor had a coherent multi-platform relational story that competed on functionality with Oracle and Sybase [study-aberdeen-1995-ibm-db2-common-server]. If a reader looks only at the entity-tagged counts, they will conclude that Aberdeen did not cover DB2. What we did was cover DB2 inside comparison and platform training work, and the future extraction pass Pete has flagged will need to surface those mentions before this section is finalized.

## What the archive won't tell you

A longitudinal book grounded in a single archive owes its reader a clear statement of what the archive does not know. Three items belong on that list. The DEC Rdb story lives inside the DEC OLTP corpus — the 1988 Blue Monday internal training, the NOTES TP1 thread, the VAXcluster debit-credit work — rather than standalone Rdb studies, and the Oracle acquisition of Rdb in 1994 is documented only through the surviving `oracle-rdb` technology tag [study-dec-blue-monday-internal-sales-training-dectp-vs-ibm-0021cc][study-dec-notes-tp1-debit-credit-thread-1987-1-5815dc]. The CCA/Model 204 story from the 1970s and early 1980s is essentially absent from the archive at one observation outside the window, and will have to be reconstructed from personal recollection and restricted sources [study-computer-corporation-of-america]. And the several 2026 synthetic longitudinal and memoir studies — the Oracle Across Four Decades assembly, the DEC longitudinal, the volume-1 memoir chapters — are scaffolding for outcome-tagging rather than period-primary evidence, and should be read that way [study-2026-kastner-oracle-longitudinal][study-volume-1-ch10-the-long-view-1966-2026].

## The through-line

What we did across the decade, when I strip the vendor detail away, was to move the buyer's frame from box selection to platform selection. The 1993 Report Card said it in one line: "IS executives changing from hardware-oriented to software-platform-oriented decision criteria" [obs-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa-shift][study-nti-6-rdbms-technology-48f4aa][ps=2]. The Jungle work handed the buyers a way to think about four otherwise indistinguishable vendors [study-jungle-1-5-675c58]. The Universal Servers piece named the next architectural chapter three years before the vendors could ship it credibly [study-aberdeen-1995-universal-servers-rdbms-technology-next-decade][ps=3]. The vendor-specific pieces — Oracle's network computing, Informix's Universal Server, Sybase's SAP face-off, DB2's Common Server, CA-Ingres's Jasmine — were where the framing met the invoice [study-aberdeen-1996-oracle-network-computing-architecture][study-informix-universal-server-launch-object-relational-fb2cd4][study-sap-sybase-face-off-f2612e][study-aberdeen-1995-ibm-db2-common-server][study-carepo-kastner-unicenter-jasmine-1996-1d5ffe]. And the OLTP and TPC work, which the following chapters take up in earnest, was the discipline that kept the vendor claims measurable [study-tandem-tr-85-2-debitcredit-1985-ca207a][study-aberdeen-open-online-transaction-process-ea272f]. That is the chapter's argument. The rest of the book is the evidence.
